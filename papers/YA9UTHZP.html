AgenticPay: A Multi-Agent LLM Negotiation System for Buyer-Seller Transactions

Contribution

The paper introduces AgenticPay, a scalable benchmark and simulation framework for evaluating large language model-based agents in multi-agent buyer-seller negotiation driven by natural language. The framework supports over 110 negotiation tasks ranging from bilateral bargaining to many-to-many markets, with dialogue-to-action grounding and welfare-oriented evaluation metrics. Agents operate under asymmetric private information, possessing reservation prices unknown to their counterparts, and negotiate through multi-turn dialogue to reach agreements. The authors benchmark state-of-the-art proprietary and open-weight LLMs under a unified protocol, revealing substantial performance gaps and persistent challenges in long-horizon strategic reasoning, particularly among smaller open-weight models.

Research Question

How effectively can current large language models function as autonomous negotiators in diverse market environments, from bilateral bargaining to complex many-to-many markets with multiple buyers, sellers, and products? What negotiation capabilities do frontier versus open-weight models possess, and where do current LLMs struggle in language-mediated economic interaction?

Method

The framework models negotiation as a finite-horizon, multi-round language game where dialogue is grounded in structured economic outcomes. Agents receive private reservation prices (buyer's maximum willingness-to-pay, seller's minimum acceptable price) injected into system prompts, while sharing public information about products and market context. At each negotiation round, agents exchange natural-language messages and extract structured negotiation actions via a parser that identifies proposed prices. Negotiation terminates when agents reach agreement (proposing the same price within the bargaining zone), exceed maximum rounds, or violate feasibility constraints. The environment supports two interaction modes: parallel negotiation where agents reason over multiple simultaneous negotiations, and sequential negotiation where agents adaptively decide whether to continue, switch, or commit to partnerships.

Setup

AgenticPay comprises 111 total negotiation tasks across eight multi-agent configurations: single buyer-seller (1B1S), single buyer with multiple sellers (1BMS), multiple buyers with single seller (MB1S), and multiple buyers with multiple sellers (MBMS), each further divided by product set complexity. Tasks span 10 realistic business scenarios organized into four economic domains—Daily Life (used smartphone, used car, vacation rental), Professional Services (website development, commercial photography, home renovation), Business Procurement (SaaS software, raw materials), and Financial Assets (luxury watch, business acquisition)—with transaction values ranging from $350 to $120,000. Agents receive identical prompts across all model evaluations under deterministic decoding (temperature 0, seed 0, max 1024 tokens per response). Benchmarked models include proprietary systems (Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.2, Gemini-3-Flash) and open-weight models (Qwen3-14B, Llama-3.1-8B).

Metrics

Three complementary outcome scores evaluate negotiation performance. GlobalScore measures overall deal quality by rewarding balanced outcomes where both parties benefit, using a symmetric quality term that peaks when surplus is split equally. BuyerScore and SellerScore capture role-specific utility, reflecting each agent's individual gain from the transaction. All three incorporate negotiation efficiency via a discount factor (γ = 0.99) that incentivizes faster agreements. Scores normalize utilities within the bargaining zone and apply a failure penalty to discourage deadlock. The framework additionally reports deal rate (percentage of successful negotiations), timeout rate, price overflow rate (proposals outside acceptable bounds), and average rounds to termination.

Results

Proprietary models substantially outperform open-weight models. Claude Opus 4.5 achieves the highest GlobalScore of 86.9 with perfect 100% deal rate and zero timeouts; Gemini-3-Flash and GPT-5.2 follow closely (82.2 and 81.7 respectively), while Qwen3-14B achieves only 63.9 with 20.7% timeout rate and Llama-3.1-8B struggles significantly with 32.5 GlobalScore and 48.6% timeouts. Negotiation efficiency inversely correlates with model capability: stronger models reach agreements faster (Claude Opus 4.5: 3.7 rounds; Llama-3.1-8B: 15.0 rounds). All models exhibit asymmetric buyer-seller performance, with systematically higher SellerScores than BuyerScores across the board. Counter-intuitively, GlobalScore increases with greater buyer and seller multiplicity, attributed to increased market liquidity enabling agents to find compatible trading partners. Financial Asset scenarios consistently expose the lowest scores across most models, suggesting sophisticated risk assessment remains challenging for current LLMs. Cross-play analysis reveals universal buyer disadvantage, with models achieving substantially higher SellerScores than BuyerScores when playing opposing roles. Personality configurations significantly impact negotiation outcomes: budget-conscious buyers paired with aggressive sellers achieve the highest GlobalScore (92.7), while busy professional buyers achieve the lowest. Sequential versus parallel negotiation modes show minimal impact on frontier models, though open-weight models benefit from parallel execution with 4-5 point improvements. Near-miss analysis reveals that over 40% of open-weight model failures occurred when price gaps were within 5 units, suggesting deficiencies in final convergence rather than understanding of the negotiation space.

Conclusion

AgenticPay establishes a foundation for studying agentic commerce and language-mediated market interaction. The benchmark reveals that strong language generation alone is insufficient for effective economic negotiation, with frontier models demonstrating substantially better negotiation performance than open-weight models. Persistent challenges in long-horizon strategic reasoning, particularly evident in financial asset negotiations and the final convergence phase, highlight areas requiring further research in autonomous LLM-based negotiators.
