Agent-to-agent negotiation

Language model agents can exchange offers for buyers, sellers, or other parties. Research benchmarks test their outcomes, failures, and safety.

Scope. Most evidence on this page comes from simulations. It does not establish performance in live consumer markets.

What the term means

In agent-to-agent negotiation, software agents bargain for people or organizations. The agents use natural-language messages to propose and discuss terms.

Many studies give each agent private limits. A buyer can receive a maximum price. A seller can receive a minimum price or cost.

The studies then record agreements, prices, utility, rule violations, or timeouts. The exact rules and measures differ across benchmarks.

How benchmark negotiations work

Multi-party benchmarks use a different structure. Several agents negotiate over several issues. Some agents can also have veto power or adversarial goals.

What the studies report

  • Model performance varies. Zhu et al. report a correlation between negotiation scores and general benchmark scores. AgenticPay also reports large gaps between tested models.
  • Weak performance can affect prices. In Zhu et al., weaker agents had more rule violations and worse financial outcomes in the simulated market.
  • Some agents fail near agreement. AgenticPay reports that more than 40% of two open-weight models' failures ended within five price units.
  • Multi-party negotiation adds safety risks. Abdelnabi et al. show that greedy or adversarial agents can change group outcomes in their games.
  • Demographic information can change outcomes. Mouri Zadeh Khaki and Choi report unequal prices across tested demographic conditions.

Capability and economic outcomes

Zhu et al. tested nine models on consumer-product simulations. They report a correlation of 0.93 between negotiation capacity and MMLU scores.

The same study reports four failure types. These are constraint violations, overpayment, deadlock, and early settlement.

In one comparison, weak sellers lost up to 14.13% of profit against strong buyers. This result applies to the study's models and setup.

AgenticPay tested more than 110 tasks. Its proprietary models scored above the tested open-weight models. Smaller models also had more timeouts.

Role asymmetry

AgenticPay reports higher seller scores than buyer scores for every tested model. The cause remains unknown.

Zhu et al. also found that seller model choice affected outcomes more than buyer model choice. These studies use different scoring systems.

Training and prompts

Zhu et al. optimized prompts for one weaker model. Its out-of-budget rate fell from 18.4% to 1.3%.

The prompt change gave limited improvement for overpayment and deadlock. The authors therefore describe this mitigation as preliminary.

Liu et al. trained a 30B buyer with reinforcement learning and verifiable economic rewards. The trained buyer scored 0.7664 on held-out products.

The strongest reported frontier baseline scored 0.4021 in that experiment. The result is specific to the paper's buyer task and reward.

The trained buyer also faced unseen and adversarial seller models. The paper reports positive surplus extraction in those tests.

Fairness and confidentiality

Mouri Zadeh Khaki and Choi tested ChatGPT-4 Turbo in 540 simulated negotiations. The agents received race and gender labels in some conditions.

Seller margins were higher for Latino buyers than for White buyers. They were also higher for female buyers than for male buyers.

Persona instructions improved buyer outcomes but did not remove relative disparities. The study did not test other model families.

Abdelnabi et al. also measured disclosure of private scores. GPT-4 disclosed none in their runs. GPT-3.5 disclosed scores in 25% of cases.

Results that depend on the setting

More agents did not have one consistent effect across studies.

  • AgenticPay reports higher welfare with more buyers and sellers. The authors attribute this result to more matching opportunities.
  • Abdelnabi et al. report lower agreement rates in harder multi-party games. Their games had several issues, thresholds, and veto parties.

These results are not a direct contradiction. The tasks change different parts of the negotiation problem.

Limits of the evidence

  • The studies use simulated agents and benchmark rules.
  • Most studies test a limited set of models, prompts, products, and opponent types.
  • Different papers use different measures of a good deal.
  • Observed associations do not identify the cause of each behavior.
  • Results can change when model versions, prompts, or market rules change.

Open questions

  • What causes the buyer disadvantage reported by AgenticPay?
  • Why do some models fail when only a small price gap remains?
  • Which safeguards work across models and market rules?
  • Do demographic disparities appear in other models and settings?
  • Can moderator agents detect sabotage without blocking valid negotiation?
  • Do verifiable-reward methods transfer to deals with subjective or long-term value?
