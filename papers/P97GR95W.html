ShoppingBench

A benchmark for shopping agents that must search products, use external knowledge, satisfy same-shop rules, and apply voucher or budget constraints.

Question

Can an agent complete realistic shopping requests when success depends on several product attributes, factual knowledge, one seller, or a voucher and budget?

The paper tests the full interaction. An agent reads an instruction, calls tools, observes results, and recommends products. The benchmark then checks the terminal recommendation against product and intent constraints.

What is new

ShoppingBench combines three elements in one end-to-end test: a catalog with more than 2.7 million products, four levels of shopping intent, and automatic checks for the final recommendation. It also uses successful GPT-4.1 tool trajectories to train a Qwen3-4B agent.

“Grounded intent” means that the request and its answer are tied to sampled products and explicit constraints in the sandbox.

Exact task and setup

250 test tasks

1. Products Finder

Find one product that matches the requested title, price range, features, and services.

150 test tasks

2. Knowledge

Answer an implicit factual question, then find a product whose title contains the required knowledge attribute. SimpleQA supplies the linked facts.

250 test tasks

3. Multi-products seller

Find all requested products and make sure that they come from one shop.

250 test tasks

4. Coupon & Budget

Find the requested product set, apply the stated voucher rules, and keep the total within the budget.

Data construction

  1. Sample real products from the Lazada catalog. For coupon tasks, also synthesize voucher rules and sample products that meet them.
  2. Extract titles, attributes, services, prices, and other fields.
  3. Use GPT-4.1 and intent-specific prompts to turn those fields into user instructions.

The full set has 3,310 instructions: 2,410 for training and 900 for testing. The four intent totals are 1,000, 310, 1,000, and 1,000 respectively.

Environment and tools

The sandbox uses BM25 product retrieval through Pyserini and web retrieval through Serper. The six tool roles are product search, product-detail view, Python calculation for discounts and budgets, web search, product recommendation, and termination.

Evaluation flow

Read the steps from 1 to 5. The order stays the same on wide and narrow screens.

  1. Receive a taskThe agent gets one intent-grounded instruction.
  2. Reason and call a toolIt emits a reasoning step and one tool action.
  3. Observe the resultThe sandbox runs the call and returns product, web, or calculation data.
  4. Repeat or stopThe loop continues until the agent calls a terminal tool.
  5. Score the productsThe evaluator compares the final recommendation with target products and intent constraints.

Metrics, with denominators

Each test task is one sample. Let n be the number of test samples for the intent. Let nᵢ be the number of products in sample i.

Product relevance, rpro

r_pro = [I(title similarity ≥ 0.5) + I(target min ≤ predicted price ≤ target max) + |target features ∩ predicted features|] / [2 + |target features|]

The numerator gives one point for title similarity, one for price, and one point for each target feature found. The denominator is the two binary checks plus the number of target features. A product gets 1 only when every component passes.

Constraint checks

  • Knowledge, rkw: 1 if the required knowledge attribute occurs in the predicted product title; otherwise 0.
  • Shop, rshop: 1 if the predicted count equals the target count and all predicted products have one unique shop ID; otherwise 0.
  • Budget, rbudget: 1 if total price is at or below the budget; otherwise 0.

CAR: partial product quality

CAR = (1 / n) × Σᵢ [(1 / nᵢ) × Σⱼ r_pro(i,j)]

For each task, average product relevance over its nᵢ products. Then average those task scores over all n tasks in that intent. CAR can give credit for a partial match.

For each intent, ASR is successful tasks divided by n. It is strict: one missed attribute can make a task fail.

Experiments and baselines

The main test compares 17 untrained language agents plus two trained Qwen3-4B variants. The reported main table uses the models’ “Think” condition.

  • Closed models: GPT-4.1, o3-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude-4-Sonnet, and Qwen2.5-max.
  • Open models: DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, Gemma-3-27B, Gemma-3-12B, and Gemma-3-4B.
  • Trained variants: SFT-Qwen3-4B and SFT+RL-Qwen3-4B.

Training comparison

GPT-4.1 produces trajectories for the 2,410 training instructions. Rejection sampling keeps only trajectories with absolute success. The AAAI paper states that this removes about half of the generated data. The retained trajectories become 5,552 step-level samples for supervised fine-tuning. The authors then use GRPO reinforcement learning with rewards for output format and matching the target tool name, parameter names, and parameter values.

Training used 8 NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs. The appendix reports 5 SFT epochs at a 5×10−5 learning rate and 5 GRPO epochs at 1×10−6.

What the analyses add

  • In a manual review of 60 failed GPT-4.1 trajectories, missing or mismatched attributes were the largest failure category.
  • Product-detail views correlate strongly with success across intents. Web-search use correlates with Knowledge success. In-shop search correlates with Multi-products seller success.
  • For GPT-4.1, “No Think” improves Products Finder ASR from 59.6% to 61.6%, but reduces Coupon & Budget ASR from 30.4% to 23.2%.
  • The human comparison covers 200 tasks and three participants. The paper reports that agents remain below humans, while humans also struggle on harder intents.

Limits and reading cautions

  • Synthetic instructions: products are real catalog entries, but GPT-4.1 writes the user requests. The benchmark does not measure naturally occurring user language.
  • One marketplace: the catalog comes from Lazada. Results may not transfer to other stores, regions, languages, inventory rules, or checkout systems.
  • Strict matching: the appendix shows that “high waist” can fail against the label “high.” ASR can therefore count a useful recommendation as wrong.
  • Dynamic external knowledge: Knowledge tasks can use Serper web search. Search results may change over time.
  • Incomplete protocol detail: the paper gives training hyperparameters, but it does not fully specify every hosted-model snapshot and inference setting. It reports no confidence intervals or repeated-run variance.
  • Product alignment is underspecified: the equations define per-product relevance and task averages, but the paper does not fully explain how predicted and target products are paired when several products are present.
  • Failure label needs care: the appendix’s “Product Missing” example says the agent stopped after finding three of four requested products. It does not show that the fourth product was absent from the catalog.

Reported-average discrepancy

Table 1 calls the final column a “domain-weighted ASR.” However, GPT-4.1’s 48.2% matches a sample-count weighted calculation over the four test sets: 250, 150, 250, and 250 tasks. The equal-intent mean of its four ASRs is 49.6%. This page keeps the reported 48.2% and states the arithmetic difference instead of choosing a new label.

Primary evidence

  1. Construction and tools: four intents, sandbox, instruction generation, and six tool roles.
  2. Evaluation: product relevance, intent constraints, CAR, and ASR equations.
  3. Experiments and Table 1: baselines, test split, and main results.
  4. Further analysis and Table 2: failures, human comparison, thinking, and web-search ablation.
  5. Appendix: product count, training settings, dataset statistics, cases, and detailed ablations.
