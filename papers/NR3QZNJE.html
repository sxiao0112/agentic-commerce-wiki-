WebShop: Towards Scalable Real-World Web Interaction with Grounded Language Agents

Shunyu Yao, Howard Chen, John Yang, and Karthik Narasimhan

WebShop is a simulated online store for training and testing agents. The agent receives a shopping request. It must search, inspect products, select options, and choose Buy. The benchmark gives an automatic score from the chosen product, its options, and its price.

Quick read

Research question

Can a realistic but controlled web store support scalable training and automatic evaluation of agents that follow natural-language shopping requests?

Core contribution

The authors combine real product text and images, high-level web actions, deterministic search, and a graded terminal reward in one reusable environment.

Best task success

29.1%

The imitation-learning agent has the highest agent Success Rate. Expert humans reach 59.6% on the same 500 test instructions.

Best average score

62.4 / 100

The imitation-plus-reinforcement-learning agent has the highest agent Task Score. Its Success Rate is 28.7%.

Do not merge these two results. Success Rate counts only episodes with a perfect reward. Task Score gives partial credit. The model with the highest Task Score is not the model with the highest Success Rate.

Terms used in the paper

Imitation learning (IL)

The model learns from actions that people took in recorded shopping sessions.

Reinforcement learning (RL)

The model updates its action policy from rewards that it receives during environment interaction.

Attribute

A desired property, such as “waterproof.” WebShop mines product attributes from product text. The agent does not see the hidden attribute set.

Option

A product choice that the agent must select, such as a color or size.

Choice oracle

An analysis tool with access to the hidden reward, attributes, and options. It exhaustively checks result items and option combinations. It takes hundreds or thousands of steps per episode. It is an upper-bound diagnostic, not a deployable agent.

What one evaluation episode does

Exact task and experimental setup

Product corpus

1,181,436 products scraped from Amazon.com. The five broad categories are beauty, food, fashion, furniture, and electronics. The main text calls the first category “makeup.” The corpus has 670 unique mined attributes and 842,849 unique options.

Instructions

12,087 crowd-written requests. Mean length is 15.9 words. Workers saw a target product, its category, attributes, and options. They were told not to copy the full title.

Split

10,587 train instructions, 1,000 development instructions, and 500 test instructions. The split is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). It does not hold out product categories.

Human data

1,012 training demonstrations support IL. Another 54 development demonstrations support model selection. Human trajectories cover all 500 test instructions.

Environment

There are four page states: search, results, item, and item detail. Page transitions and retrieval are deterministic. Humans use HTML mode. Models use a text-focused simple mode.

Reported trials

Figure 4 reports the model results over three trials on the 500-item test set. The paper reports means in the main comparison but does not print a confidence interval in the figure text.

Sources: §3.2, §5.1, Figure 4, Appendix A.1, and Table 6 of arXiv v4.

Metrics and denominators

Let A be the requested attributes, O the requested options, and B the price budget. Let the selected product supply the matched sets and the actual price.

episode reward = type multiplier × (matched attributes + matched options + price-within-budget indicator) ÷ (number of requested attributes + number of requested options + 1)

Task Score

100 × the mean episode reward. The denominator is the number of evaluated episodes. On the main test, that is 500. A partially correct purchase can raise this score.

Success Rate

Episodes with reward exactly 1 ÷ evaluated instructions. On the main test, that is the count of perfect-reward episodes divided by 500. A different product can count as success if it satisfies the request.

Attribute and option subscores

For one episode, each is the number of matched requested values divided by the number of requested values of that kind. Table 2 reports their average values on a 0–100 scale.

Price and type subscores

Price is 1 when the selected price is at or below the budget and 0 otherwise. Type is a 0, 0.1, 0.5, or 1 multiplier based on title and category matching. Table 2 averages each component on a 0–100 scale.

The reward gives one denominator slot to price. Attributes and options each contribute one slot per requested value. The type score multiplies the full fraction. The pinned reward implementation also uses fuzzy token matching for attribute and option values.

Metric limit: the automatic matcher can miss synonyms. In Appendix A.7, manual rescoring was higher than automatic scoring. The authors describe the automatic reward as a reasonable lower bound for the sampled human trajectories.

Systems and comparisons

  • Rule: search with the full instruction, choose the first result, select no options, and buy.
  • IL: a fine-tuned BART model generates search queries. A BERT-based policy scores click actions. A ResNet-50 supplies image features.
  • IL without language pre-training: ablations remove pre-trained weights from the choice model or replace the learned search generator with the rule query.
  • RL: policy-gradient training starts from pre-trained BERT but has no IL warm start.
  • RL (RNN): an RL baseline uses bidirectional GRU text encoders.
  • IL+RL: online RL fine-tunes the IL choice policy. The BART search model stays frozen and provides ten query candidates.
  • Human expert: the top seven of 13 qualified workers form the expert pool. Five experts took part in the main human evaluation.
  • Choice oracle: hidden-state exhaustive analysis. Use it to locate a bottleneck. Do not compare its cost with the learned agents as if it were a practical baseline.

Training detail: IL uses 10 epochs, effective batch size 32, and learning rate 2×10−5. Transformer RL uses four environments for 100,000 steps and learning rate 10−5. See Appendix C.1–C.2.

RL changes the trade-off

From IL to IL+RL, Task Score rises from 59.9 to 62.4. The option subscore falls from 45.2 to 38.9. Mean visited states fall from 9.4 to 4.5 in Table 2. The authors interpret this as more greedy and less exploratory behavior.

Option choice is a major bottleneck

The human option subscore is 73.9. The IL score is 45.2, a 28.7-point gap. In corrected v4 Table 4, the exhaustive Choice oracle reaches 84.2% to 87.8% Success Rate, depending on the search query source.

Language pre-training matters

Figure 4 reports 29.1% Success Rate for IL and 10.6% when the choice Transformer starts without language pre-training. Replacing learned search with the rule query gives 26.3%.

Images add little in this setup

Appendix Table 10 reports 60.6 Score and 29.0% Success Rate for IL, versus 60.3 and 28.4% without images. The authors link this small gap to instructions and rewards that use text.

Reward audit

Appendix A.7 manually re-scores 100 average-worker trajectories and 100 expert trajectories. Automatic versus manual Task Score is 74.9 versus 76.3 for average workers, with Pearson correlation 0.856. It is 81.5 versus 89.9 for experts, with correlation 0.773. For average workers, 87% of automatic scores are within 10 points of the manual score.

What the evidence supports

  • Supported: WebShop provides a large controlled test of search, product comparison, option selection, and terminal choice.
  • Supported: learned IL and IL+RL agents outperform the paper's rule baseline on both reported main metrics.
  • Supported: the best agent result depends on the metric. IL has higher perfect completion. IL+RL has higher average graded reward.
  • Supported as diagnosis: corrected Choice-oracle results show that action and option selection leave large room for improvement when the search results are fixed.
  • Author claim: the real-site experiment shows “non-trivial” transfer. A narrower description is that the trained policies retained useful navigation behavior under two site adapters and manual scoring.

Limits and version notes

  • Corrected oracle values: arXiv v4 Table 4 reports 84.2%–87.8% Success Rate. The NeurIPS camera-ready PDF reports 52.6%–61.0%. The arXiv record says v4 fixes this result. This page uses v4.
  • Only an i.i.d. split: the paper does not test category-held-out generalization. It names this as future work.
  • Source count conflict: §3.2 says 113 subcategory queries. Appendix A.1 says 313 subcategory names. The paper does not resolve the difference.
  • Trajectory count conflict: Table 2 gives 4.5 mean visited states for IL+RL. The §5.3 prose says the trajectory length drops to 4.8. This page uses the table value when it discusses states.
  • Reward mismatch: exact and fuzzy text rules can miss synonyms. The audit finds a larger shortfall for expert trajectories.
  • Limited representation: the products come from the United States English-language site and five broad categories. Manual attribute filtering may add annotator bias.
  • Task artifacts: some attributes are generic and some option strings are unusually specific. An agent can use a rare option as a retrieval cue.
  • Limited visual need: instructions and rewards use textual product information. The image ablation changes results only slightly.
  • Real-site scope: transfer uses 100 instructions per site and manual scoring. Only navigation reaches the live sites. The transfer interface contains option and Buy actions.
  • No deployment evidence: the study does not measure payment, consent, returns, delivery, fraud, or safe autonomous purchasing.
