What Is Your AI Agent Buying? Evaluation, Biases, Model Dependence, & Emerging Implications for Agentic E-Commerce

Contribution

The authors develop ACES (Agentic e-CommercE Simulator), a provider-agnostic evaluation framework for auditing how frontier AI shopping agents select products. The framework combines a multimodal large language model with a fully controllable mock e-commerce platform to conduct randomized experiments that causally isolate the effects of product attributes and platform levers on agent choice. By evaluating Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4.1, and Gemini 2.5 Flash (and their successors as of December 2025), the authors document that AI agents exhibit strong position biases, concentration risk through choice homogeneity, substantial sensitivity to platform endorsements while penalizing sponsored tags, and exploitable vulnerabilities to seller-side listing optimization. The framework demonstrates that the rise of AI-mediated shopping creates a fundamentally different demand surface from human-centric commerce, with market shares that are volatile, highly model-dependent, and susceptible to upstream model updates as exogenous demand shocks.

Research Question

The authors investigate whether AI agents shopping on behalf of consumers behave as perfectly rational economic actors or whether they exhibit unique, exploitable biases. More specifically, they ask: (1) Do agents satisfy basic instruction following and economic rationality? (2) What market shares emerge under full AI mediation and how do they vary across models? (3) How do agents trade off product attributes and respond to position, endorsements, and other platform levers? (4) Can sellers strategically respond to AI buyers through listing optimization? (5) How do these behaviors persist across model providers, model generations, interface modalities, and prompt variations?

Method

ACES consists of three components: a browser-use agent (implemented with LangChain and Selenium) powered by a vision language model, a fully controllable mock e-commerce platform with eight product categories and eight products per category, and a streamlined experimental workflow (Veni–Vidi–Emi: open, search, screenshot, declare choice). The platform renders product listings in a fixed 2×4 grid with no detail pages, ensuring all information needed for choice is visible. The authors standardized inference settings to minimal reasoning depth across all models to reflect real-time e-commerce latency constraints and set temperature to 1.0. For instruction-following and rationality tests, they create choice sets with a single objectively correct option (budget constraint, specified color, lowest price, or highest rating) and run 50 trials per task with randomized positions. For choice-behavior analysis, they generate 500 scenarios per product category with exogenous randomization of product position, sponsored/endorsement/scarcity tags, prices, ratings, and review counts, then estimate conditional logit models to recover causal choice sensitivities. For seller-response analysis, they prompt a seller-side AI agent (GPT-4.1 or GPT-5.1) to optimize product descriptions given competitor sales data, re-run 200 purchase experiments with the modified description, and measure the causal change in market share by reusing identical product position shuffles in pre/post experiments.

Setup

Experiments span eight product categories (fitness watches, iPhone 16 Pro covers, mousepads, office lamps, staplers, toilet paper, toothpaste, washing machines) selected to represent high/low-ticket items and diverse brands. State-of-the-art models evaluated in August 2025: Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash (600 instruction-following and rationality trials per model). Choice-behavior experiments: 500 exogenous scenarios per category per model (4,000 choice sets total per model, pooled across categories). Headless/API experiments: same randomization, agents respond with ranked JSON instead of screenshots. Prompt variations: default, ignore-position, and ignore-position-and-prioritize-price across both UI and headless modalities. Seller-response experiments: one focal product per category, 200 position-shuffled baseline trials, one-shot AI-generated description edit, 200 post-modification trials. Latest models (December 2025) re-evaluated: Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.1, Gemini 3.0 Pro Preview.

Results

Baseline Rationality: Frontier models as of December 2025 (Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.1, Gemini 3.0 Pro Preview) achieve near-zero error rates on instruction-following and basic economic rationality tasks, representing substantial improvement from August 2025 versions. For example, models fail <1.7% of rating tests where a product is marginally better, down from 15-29% for August 2025 models.

Product Market Shares: Different AI models induce highly distinct market shares for identical product assortments. Example: Fitbit Inspire selected 45% of the time by Claude Sonnet 4, 25% by GPT-4.1, 39.4% by Gemini 2.5 Flash. After model upgrades, the same product's share jumps to 77% (Claude Opus 4.5) or crashes to 6% (GPT-5.1). Agents exhibit choice homogeneity, collapsing demand onto a few modal products and creating concentration risk (e.g., Amazon Basics staplers: 80-94% across models, Arrow never selected). Market shares are unstable: model finalization (Gemini 2.5 Flash Preview to Gemini 2.5 Flash) inverts position biases and reshuffles modal products across categories.

Position Effects: All three models assign significant utility premiums to top-row placement, but the direction and magnitude differ sharply across providers and even across models from the same provider. GPT-4.1 prefers column 1, Claude Sonnet 4 prefers columns 2-3, Gemini 2.5 Flash prefers column 4. Position changes yield 5-7 fold swings in product selection probability. Strikingly, GPT-4.1 and GPT-5.1 exhibit almost opposite position biases (one's preferred position is the other's least preferred). Position effects persist in headless/API settings with no images and resist correction via explicit prompting.

Badge Effects: All six focal models penalize sponsored tags (8-24% reduction in selection probability relative to baseline) while rewarding platform overall-pick endorsements substantially (3-5x selection probability lift). This echoes human behavior: agents discount advertising but treat platform endorsement as a credibility signal.

Product Attributes: All models assign negative weight to price and positive weight to ratings and review count, aligning with rational preferences. However, sensitivities differ sharply across models. Price elasticity estimates range -1.6 to -2.2 (consistent with human price elasticity for brand/SKU level). A +0.1 rating increase lifts selection probability to 15-20% from a 10% baseline across models.

Seller Response: Modest description edits can drive substantial market-share gains. One-shot AI-optimized descriptions yield statistically significant increases in 33% of category-model pairs, with gains ranging +3.66 to +14.89 percentage points on average. In office-lamp category, front-loading the keyword "Office" (which was truncated in the original description) drives massive gains: GPT-5.1 +80.4 p.p., Gemini 2.5 Flash +52.0 p.p., Claude Opus 4.5 +41.0 p.p. Gains are driven by keyword front-loading, category injection, feature reordering, and specification enrichment. However, interventions are not uniformly effective across models: a description optimized for one model may backfire for another (e.g., Gemini 3.0 Pro Preview mousepad -12.5 p.p.), highlighting the brittleness and volatility of agentic markets.

Robustness Analysis: (1) Temporal drift within model version (August to September 2025) shows minimal significant change for Claude Sonnet 4 and GPT-4.1, but Gemini 2.5 Flash shows a statistically significant increase in top-row preference. (2) Model finalization acts as an exogenous demand shock, inverting position biases and reallocating market share. (3) Explicit prompting to ignore position reduces but does not eliminate position effects; prompting to prioritize price steepens price sensitivity but position effects remain economically meaningful. (4) Headless experiments with ranked JSON lists (no images) reproduce similar position biases and badge effects, indicating these are not artifacts of visual processing but deep-seated model properties.

Conclusion

AI shopping agents are not perfectly rational optimizers but exhibit systematic, heterogeneous, and often unstable choice patterns. Market shares are volatile and highly model-dependent; model updates function as exogenous demand shocks. Position biases are large, model-specific, and persist across interfaces and prompt variations, suggesting they are intrinsic to model behavior rather than surface artifacts. Agents penalize sponsorship but reward platform endorsements, creating new monetization and design challenges for platforms. Sellers face continuous pressure to adapt listings to evolving agent behavior (AI-SEO or generative engine optimization). The authors position ACES as a framework for Algorithmic Economic Auditing—a necessary infrastructure for market governance as AI mediates increasingly more commerce. They call for continuous monitoring and evaluation of agents by platforms and regulators, development of public dashboards publishing agent choice profiles, and further investigation into the mechanisms driving biases (pre-training vs. post-training, vision encoder, model drift).

Related Work

Computer-Use Agents and Benchmarks

WebArena introduces reproducible long-horizon web tasks over functional sites with baseline agents achieving low end-to-end success relative to humans — WebArena, Zhou et al. — foundational work on web-task evaluation for agents, which the authors build upon by isolating the product-selection choice step for fine-grained causal analysis.

VisualWebArena extends to multimodal interfaces with images and forms — VisualWebArena, Koh et al. — advances web agent evaluation to visual modalities; ACES applies similar multimodal evaluation but focuses on shopping choice rather than end-to-end task completion.

Mind2Web targets generalist web agents across 137 real websites — Mind2Web, Deng et al. — establishes breadth of web agent capabilities; ACES constrains scope to isolate choice behavior cleanly.

OSWorld provides a real-computer environment (Ubuntu, Windows, macOS) and documents large headroom between state-of-the-art agents and humans — OSWorld, Xie et al. — extends agent benchmarking to operating systems; ACES applies similar rigor to e-commerce choice.

SWE-agent studies how UI design and tool affordances affect agent success — SWE-agent, Yang et al. — demonstrates importance of interface design for agent success; ACES systematically varies interface modality (UI vs. headless) to test robustness.

UI-TARS and Agent S2 report architectural advances (GUI grounding, hierarchical planning) — UI-TARS and Agent S2, Zhao et al. and Agashe et al. — showcase emerging GUI-agent technical approaches; ACES uses established LangChain-based architecture to focus on behavioral measurement.

Autonomous Shopping Agents and Multimodal Product Understanding

WebShop frames shopping as instruction-following in a simulated web store, training agents with imitation and reinforcement learning — WebShop, Yao et al. — establishes the shopping-agent task; ACES builds an evaluation framework for a narrower choice-based task without training agents.

Shopping MMLU and DeepShop provide text-based and deep-research shopping benchmarks — Shopping MMLU (Jin et al.) and DeepShop (Lyu et al.) — evaluate multi-task retail skills and live-navigation; ACES focuses on causal choice behavior under controlled randomization.

Dialog-driven and domain-tuned LLMs for e-commerce (eCeLLM, LiLiuM) — eCeLLM (Peng et al.) and LiLiuM (Herold et al.) — specialize LLMs for e-commerce; ACES evaluates frontier general-purpose models directly without domain tuning.

Vision-language product understanding (PUMGPT) — PUMGPT, Xue et al. — addresses attribute extraction from images and text; ACES uses VLMs directly as shopping agents without auxiliary product-understanding modules.

Product Rankings, Platform Design, and Assortment Optimization

Rankings causally shape consumer examination and purchase behavior — Ursu, Raluca — empirical evidence on ranking effects in human behavior; ACES replicates this in agent behavior, finding comparable elasticity but model-specific bias directions.

Ranking and ratings have direct and interaction effects on consumer behavior and platform revenue — Ghose et al. — studies human choice under ranking and ratings; ACES measures analogous effects for agents and finds higher heterogeneity.

Theoretical and empirical work on modeling consumer search and platform ranking algorithms — Compiani et al. and Derakhshan et al. — develop theory and empirics of search and ranking; ACES provides agent-centric empirical estimates of position sensitivity for marketplace design.

Assortment optimization under nested logit and related choice models — Davis et al., Désir et al., Goyal et al. — classical assortment optimization; ACES supplies agent choice model estimates (via conditional logit) that can feed assortment problems.

Platform Endorsements and Badges

Badges (Best Seller, Overall Pick, scarcity labels) meaningfully change clicks and add-to-cart probabilities in human shoppers — Lill et al. — establishes human sensitivity to badges; ACES measures analogous agent sensitivity and finds stronger overall-pick effects and consistent sponsor-penalization.

Badge longevity, uniformity, and bias across marketplaces — Bansal et al. — examines badge deployment; ACES quantifies causal effects of badges on agent choice.

Causal effects of badges in online communities and social status — Immorlica et al. and Kusmierczyk and Gomez-Rodriguez — study badge impact in non-shopping contexts; ACES applies badge evaluation to shopping agents.

LLMs for Simulating Human Responses and Market Research

LLMs as silicon samples to simulate human behavior and preferences, validated against human survey data — Brand et al., Horton, Goli and Singh — investigate LLM fidelity to human behavior; ACES explicitly treats AI agents as distinct decision-makers rather than human proxies, measuring their autonomous choice function.

Data augmentation via LLMs for market analysis — Wang et al. — uses LLMs to enhance market analysis; ACES measures agent behavior as a direct input to market modeling.

Digital-twin style behavioral modeling — Peng et al. — models personalized human preferences; ACES uses generic prompts to characterize baseline agent behavior prior to personalization.

Personalization and Recommender Systems

Classical recommender systems and personalization's role in e-commerce — Ricci et al. — foundational recommender systems; ACES deliberately avoids pre-specified personalization to measure baseline agent priors.

LLM personalization benchmarks and methods for tailoring responses to user preferences — Zollo et al. — develops LLM personalization; ACES evaluates unpersonalized agents as a baseline.

Algorithmic Delegation

Theoretical work on delegation studies how principals can design mechanisms when agents have misaligned incentives or information — Armstrong and Vickers — models delegation under misalignment; ACES empirically documents outcome of delegation to AI agents in a concrete marketplace.

Appropriately constrained delegation can approximate efficient search despite misaligned incentives — Kleinberg and Kleinberg — theory of delegated search; ACES reveals empirical volatility that complicates efficient delegation.

Benefits of delegating search to multiple agents — Hajiaghayi et al. — shows advantages of multi-agent delegation; ACES documents heterogeneity across agents that motivates multi-agent strategies.

Optimal design of algorithmic delegates under information asymmetry — Greenwood et al. — designs delegate mechanisms; ACES provides empirical measurements of current delegate behavior.

Related Work on Model Behavior and Bias

Race and gender disparities in AI-based hiring show biases persist across variations, paralleling the authors' findings on shopping agents — cited as Gaarlandt et al., Furr, Shipilov; demonstrates broader pattern of exploitable AI biases across domains.

Post-training significantly impacts model biases, as evidenced by Gemini 2.5 Flash finalization effects — discussed throughout ACES results, echoes findings in model fine-tuning literature.

Vision encoders and model drift are less significant drivers of observed biases than post-training — authors' phenomenological analysis of mechanisms.
