Generative Engine Optimization and Search Manipulation

Generative engine optimization (GEO) changes content to improve its visibility in a generative engine. In commerce, the target can be a product's position in an LLM-generated recommendation list.

Some methods rewrite useful product content. Other methods insert adversarial text. The studies on this page test both types of intervention.

Evidence scope. The three studies use different systems, models, data, and success measures. They do not provide a direct comparison between methods. The E-GEO details below use arXiv v2, dated 14 July 2026.

Where content can affect the system

A shopping engine can retrieve candidate products and then ask an LLM to rank them. The papers test two separate surfaces in this process.

What the current evidence shows

Readable rewrites can change product rank

Bagga et al. (2026, arXiv v2) introduce E-GEO. The dataset contains 13,747 conversational product queries. Each query has 10 retrieved Amazon listings.

The study keeps each 10-product candidate set fixed. It tests changes at the LLM re-ranking stage. The evaluation covers five LLM re-rankers, seven rewriting models, and 15 hand-written rewriting heuristics.

The authors also optimize the rewriting prompt across several re-rankers. The optimized prompts improve the authors' aggregate objective more than the heuristic starting prompts. Results still vary by re-ranker and prompt. The paper calls the shared pattern a “universally effective” strategy, but this is the authors' interpretation within their benchmark.

The v2 paper also tests adversarial rewrites. Under its simple prompt-based defense, overt attacks lose effectiveness. This result applies to the paper's fixed-candidate re-ranking setup.

Strategic text can steer one controlled recommender

Kumar and Lakkaraju (2024, arXiv v2) add an optimized strategic text sequence to a product record. They use the Greedy Coordinate Gradient method to make the target product appear first.

The experiment uses Llama-2, 10 fictitious coffee machines, one affordability query, and two target products. One target moves from absent to first in the fixed-order setup. The authors run 200 evaluations to test changes in product order.

A sequence trained on one fixed product order gives the first target an advantage in about 40% of randomized-order evaluations. Training with random product orders improves robustness. The paper reports the same direction of effect for the second target.

Poisoned passages can steer dense retrieval

Ben-Tov and Sharif (2025, arXiv v2; ACM CCS 2025) introduce GASLITE. The method inserts passages into a corpus without changing the retriever. Each passage keeps attacker-chosen information and adds an optimized trigger.

The study tests nine dense retrievers. Most experiments use the 8.8-million-passage MSMARCO corpus. Success means that at least one adversarial passage appears in the top k results.

  • Known query: The attack reaches 100% appeared@1 across the nine tested retrievers.
  • Known topic: A small sample of topic queries guides the attack. Ten passages equal about 0.0001% of MSMARCO. The paper reports more than 50% mean appeared@10 for most tested models.
  • Broad query set: The attack needs a larger budget and produces lower success than the topic-specific attack.

The authors find large differences between retrievers. They associate some differences with the similarity function and embedding-space geometry. They describe the anisotropy link as a hypothesis.

How to interpret the three studies

  • E-GEO measures product-description rewriting after retrieval.
  • Kumar and Lakkaraju measure adversarial product text in one controlled LLM recommendation setup.
  • GASLITE measures corpus poisoning in dense retrieval. It does not measure the final purchasing decision.
  • All three show that text can affect visibility in their tested settings.
  • The studies do not establish one mechanism, one effect size, or one result that applies to every deployed shopping engine.

Limits and uncertainty

  • E-GEO: The authors model the ranking component with fixed candidate sets. They do not test a full production shopping system. The dataset focuses on durable-goods requests from one subreddit.
  • Strategic text sequences: The study uses a small fictitious catalog and Llama-2. It does not show the same effect across deployed commerce platforms.
  • GASLITE: The study focuses on worst-case robustness. The optimization is computationally demanding. Most evaluated retrievers are encoder models near the BERT-base scale.
  • Market effects: None of the three studies measures completed purchases, long-term seller behavior, or market-wide adoption.

Open questions

  • Do the reported effects hold in production shopping systems with changing catalogs and hidden ranking rules?
  • Which defenses work across both retrieval attacks and re-ranking attacks?
  • How can a system distinguish useful product editing from manipulative text?
  • What happens when many sellers optimize content at the same time?
  • How do these methods affect seller access, recommendation quality, and user welfare?
  • Do text-based findings extend to product images, video, and other media?
