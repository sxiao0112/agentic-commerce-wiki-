Agent-to-agent negotiation

Autonomous LLM agents bargaining and transacting on behalf of buyers and sellers, and the benchmarks that measure their negotiation skill, risks, and safety.

Definition

Agent-to-agent negotiation studies what happens when both sides of a deal delegate their bargaining to AI agents that exchange natural-language messages to reach an agreement Zhu et al., 2025.

The core setting is a multi-round language game grounded in structured economic outcomes Liu et al., 2026. Agents hold private information their counterparts cannot see. A buyer knows its budget or maximum willingness-to-pay; a seller knows its wholesale cost or minimum acceptable price Zhu et al., 2025 Liu et al., 2026. They negotiate through multi-turn dialogue, and a deal forms when the proposed prices fall within the bargaining zone Liu et al., 2026.

The interaction is commonly framed as competitive. Zhu et al. model buyer-seller negotiation as an information-incomplete zero-sum game where each side optimizes the final transaction price within feasibility constraints Zhu et al., 2025. Liu et al. instead score deals by welfare, rewarding outcomes where both parties benefit and surplus is split evenly, alongside role-specific buyer and seller utility Liu et al., 2026.

Negotiation also extends beyond two parties. Abdelnabi et al. define scorable multi-party games where five to seven agents with secret scoring systems and acceptance thresholds must agree on multi-issue deals, with a default fallback payoff when no agreement is reached Abdelnabi et al., 2024. In this view negotiation is a composite skill: it demands arithmetic, theory-of-mind inference of other agents' preferences, multi-turn planning, and resistance to manipulation Abdelnabi et al., 2024.

State of the area (synthesis)

The collected papers converge on one finding. Negotiation ability tracks general model capability. Zhu et al. report that negotiation capacity correlates very strongly with MMLU (r=0.93) and substantially with mathematical ability (r=0.87), and that larger models within a family negotiate better Zhu et al., 2025. Liu et al. find the same gap: proprietary frontier models substantially outperform smaller open-weight models, which time out and fail to converge far more often Liu et al., 2026. Abdelnabi et al. show GPT-4 reaching agreement in 81% of base-game runs while GPT-3.5 reaches only 20%, with weaker models failing on arithmetic and theory of mind Abdelnabi et al., 2024.

This capability gap is not a benign quality difference. It is an economic one. Zhu et al. frame automated agent-to-agent negotiation as an inherently imbalanced game where the party using the weaker model loses money Zhu et al., 2025. Weak buyers facing strong sellers overpay; weak sellers lose up to roughly 14% profit against strong buyers Zhu et al., 2025. The same asymmetry appears in mixed populations elsewhere: Abdelnabi et al. find that the same agent role earns lower payoffs when paired with weaker models, and that including a GPT-3.5 agent lowers success for everyone Abdelnabi et al., 2024.

The capability gap need not be permanent, and training can close it. Liu et al. show that a mid-sized model trained with reinforcement learning from verifiable economic rewards beats frontier-scale models on the same bilateral bargaining task Liu et al., 2026. Their reward is grounded in objective outcomes rather than human preferences: for a successful transaction it equals the buyer's surplus normalized by the gap between budget and seller cost, scaled to a fixed range, so the signal rewards surplus extraction and penalizes deals that exceed the buyer's budget Liu et al., 2026. Training a Qwen3-30B buyer with Group Relative Policy Optimization against a regulated seller, they reach a reward of 0.7664 on held-out products, nearly double the strongest frontier baseline and more than double larger reasoning models Liu et al., 2026. This challenges the notion that strong negotiation belongs only to the largest models, complicating the bigger-is-better picture the benchmarks report Liu et al., 2026.

Both benchmarks of bilateral consumer bargaining also surface concrete failure modes rather than just lower scores. Zhu et al. name four behavioral anomalies that translate into financial risk: constraint violation (accepting deals over budget or below wholesale), excessive payment (overpaying past retail when budgets are disclosed early), negotiation deadlock, and early settlement by buyers with slack budgets Zhu et al., 2025. Liu et al. independently report a final-convergence problem: over 40% of open-weight failures occur when the remaining price gap is within five units, a deficiency in closing rather than in understanding the bargaining space Liu et al., 2026. Liu et al.'s RL training directly targets two of these anomalies: it cuts the trained buyer's price-overshoot rate to 0.10% from 10.35% for the untrained baseline, and it suppresses the politeness-driven concessions and artificial empathy that lead instruction-tuned models to give value away Liu et al., 2026.

Disparities in outcome can also track who the counterpart is, not just how capable each model is. Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi run a buyer-seller game adapted from NegotiationArena where one side is told the other's gender and race, and find that demographic awareness alone produces systematically unequal deals Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. A seller aware of buyer demographics extracts higher profit margins from Latino and female buyers than from White and male buyers, with the seller's initial offer strongly predicting the final price Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. The bias surfaces as subtle shifts in offers, counteroffers, and concession timing rather than any explicit discriminatory language in the reasoning traces, which the authors attribute to hidden gender and social biases in training data Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. This adds a demographic dimension to the economic stakes the bilateral benchmarks already raise: the weaker party is not the only one who can lose money.

A persistent and unexplained pattern is a buyer-versus-seller asymmetry. Liu et al. find systematically higher seller scores than buyer scores across every model and in cross-play, a universal buyer disadvantage they cannot yet attribute to either training-data bias toward persuasive selling or to the structure of the reward Liu et al., 2026. Zhu et al. report a complementary effect from the market side: the seller model choice affects outcomes more than the buyer model choice Zhu et al., 2025. Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi see a role-dependent advantage too, finding that the side made aware of the other's demographics gains, so buyers told the seller's demographics beat the blind baseline across every seller subpopulation Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. The papers describe these asymmetries in different terms and none resolves its cause.

Trained strategy also appears to generalize rather than overfit its training opponent. Liu et al. find their RL-trained buyer keeps a positive edge against an unseen stronger frontier seller, and holds positive surplus extraction against adversarial seller personas that beg, insult, or refuse to yield Liu et al., 2026. They trace the strategy's emergence through a four-phase behavioral evolution: aggressive anchoring, temporary deadlock, rational concession, and finally multi-turn linguistic persuasion at a high deal rate Liu et al., 2026. This positions verifiable-reward RL as one route to the strategic rationality and manipulation resistance that the multi-party safety line also prizes Abdelnabi et al., 2024.

The papers diverge on whether more agents help or harm. Liu et al. find that deal welfare counter-intuitively increases with greater buyer and seller multiplicity, attributing it to market liquidity that lets agents find compatible partners Liu et al., 2026. Abdelnabi et al. instead find that adding parties and tightening the feasible-deal ratio sharply degrades success, dropping GPT-4 from 81% to 30% as feasible deals shrink Abdelnabi et al., 2024. The settings differ: liquidity in a price-only market eases matching, while multi-issue games with veto parties make agreement combinatorially harder.

Safety is the distinctive concern of the multi-party line. Abdelnabi et al. show that a single greedy agent biases final deals toward its own preferences and that an adversarial agent instructed to sabotage can depress group success and target specific parties for worse outcomes Abdelnabi et al., 2024. This connects negotiation to broader worries about manipulation and adversarial agents studied under agent-security-and-prompt-injection. Their score-leakage metric, measuring whether agents reveal confidential thresholds, also makes confidentiality a negotiation property, with GPT-4 leaking nothing and GPT-3.5 leaking in a quarter of cases Abdelnabi et al., 2024.

On mitigation, the field divides between prompt-level and training-level fixes, and the training side now looks stronger. Zhu et al. show that reinforcement-learning-based prompt optimization sharply cuts out-of-budget errors for a weak model, from 18.4% to 1.3%, but barely helps with overpayment or deadlock Zhu et al., 2025. They recommend human-in-the-loop oversight for financially vulnerable users Zhu et al., 2025. Liu et al. push further, updating model parameters rather than prompts, and report that verifiable-reward RL both internalizes strategic discipline and nearly eliminates the budget violations that prompt tuning only partly reduces Liu et al., 2026. Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi test persona-based conditioning, where a disadvantaged buyer is told to appear desperate and plead, and find it substantially improves buyer outcomes yet leaves the relative demographic disparities intact, so conditioning enhances performance within a biased system rather than removing the bias Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. They warn that this shifts the burden of fairness onto disadvantaged users and argue for model-level fixes such as dataset curation and demographic masking Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. Abdelnabi et al. point instead to structural defenses such as moderator agents and coalition-aware mechanisms Abdelnabi et al., 2024. The economic stakes of these capability gaps link this field to ai-mediated-market-dynamics and to the two-sided settings of agentic-marketplaces.

Papers in this field

  • Zhu et al., 2025 — models and benchmarks agent-to-agent negotiation and transactions in consumer markets, showing automation is an imbalanced game where weaker-model parties face systematic economic losses through four behavioral anomalies.
  • Liu et al., 2026 — a scalable benchmark of 110+ language-mediated negotiation tasks from bilateral bargaining to many-to-many markets, revealing capability gaps, a universal buyer disadvantage, and final-convergence deficiencies in open-weight models.
  • Liu et al., 2026 — a reinforcement-learning framework grounded in verifiable economic rewards that trains a 30B LLM buyer to negotiate strategically, more than doubling surplus extraction over frontier models, eliminating budget violations, and generalizing to stronger and adversarial sellers.
  • Abdelnabi et al., 2024 — a scorable multi-party, multi-issue negotiation benchmark testing cooperation, competition, and maliciousness, showing that even GPT-4 struggles on constrained and adversarial games and that greedy or rogue agents can unfairly alter outcomes.
  • Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026 — a multi-agent buyer-seller game evaluating demographic fairness in ChatGPT-4 Turbo negotiators, showing that gender and racial awareness produces systematically unequal outcomes through subtle behavioral shifts and that persona-based conditioning mitigates but does not remove the disparity.

Contradictions

  • Effect of more agents on agreement: Liu et al. find deal welfare rises with greater buyer and seller multiplicity due to market liquidity Liu et al., 2026, whereas Abdelnabi et al. find that adding parties and tightening feasible-deal ratios sharply lowers agreement success Abdelnabi et al., 2024. The settings differ (price-only matching versus multi-issue games with veto parties), so the disagreement is contextual rather than direct.

Open questions / gaps

  • The cause of the systematic buyer-versus-seller asymmetry is unresolved: whether it stems from training-data bias toward persuasive selling or from the negotiation reward structure Liu et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Why financial-asset and high-stakes scenarios consistently expose the lowest scores lacks a mechanistic explanation; sophisticated risk assessment seems to be the bottleneck but is unverified Liu et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Whether the final-convergence failure on near-miss deals is a reasoning, generation, or confidence-calibration problem is not investigated Liu et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Mitigation remains preliminary: RL-based prompt optimization reduces budget violations but not overpayment or deadlock, and systematic safety-and-performance mitigation for real platforms is open Zhu et al., 2025. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Whether demographic bias in negotiator agents is specific to ChatGPT-4 Turbo or general across other LLMs is untested, and model-level mitigation that does not shift the fairness burden onto disadvantaged users remains undeveloped Mouri Zadeh Khaki & Choi, 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Structural defenses are untested: moderator agents to detect intruders, evasion attacks against such moderators, private channels for coalition formation, and fairness when agents of differing capability interact Abdelnabi et al., 2024. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Whether verifiable-reward RL extends to negotiation settings with less objectively measurable outcomes or costly compliance verification is untested, as is the transferability of learned strategies across product categories, multi-party deals, and reputation-sensitive markets Liu et al., 2026.
