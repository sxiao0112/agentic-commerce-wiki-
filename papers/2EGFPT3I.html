AgenticShop: Benchmarking Agentic Product Curation for Personalized Web Shopping

A benchmark for testing whether web-enabled agents can find products on the open web and match them to a person’s stated preferences.

Research question

How well can search-enabled language models and autonomous web agents curate products from the open web that satisfy a specific user’s preferences across realistic shopping intents?

What this paper adds

AgenticShop joins three elements in one evaluation: open-web product discovery, user profiles derived from real Amazon purchase and review histories, and item-level personalization checks grounded in the pages that agents cite. The paper presents it as the first benchmark for personalized product curation in an open-web setting. §1

Plain definition: product curation means finding a set of candidate products and selecting options that fit a user’s context. It requires both discovery and preference matching.

Task and evaluation flow

  1. Build a user profileUse a person’s category-focused purchase history and reviews to create a narrative context, an intent-specific query, and a checklist.
  2. Give the task to an agentThe agent receives the context and query, then explores the open web. Open Exploration has no explicit product query.
  3. Return product candidatesThe output is a list L of M products with linked pages.
  4. Extract page evidencePlaywright renders each linked page. A web-enabled extractor receives its URL and viewport screenshots and extracts product information.
  5. Judge every product × criterion pairGPT-5-mini assigns 1 when extracted evidence satisfies a checklist item and 0 otherwise. The score averages these decisions.

Benchmark setup: 350 tasks

The authors select users with at least 100 reviews, find each user’s most purchased subcategory, and retain history from that target product type. GPT-5-mini turns product metadata and reviews into the narrative context and six-dimensional checklist: brand, price, reviews, function, aesthetics, and purchase conditions such as seller or shipping.

Metrics and denominators

Personalized curation score

Score = [Σu∈U Σm=1…M Σk=1…K J(I(rm), Ck)] ÷ (|U| × M × K)

  • |U|: number of users/tasks in the evaluated scenario (50 in each scenario).
  • M: product candidates returned for a user; K: checklist criteria for that user.
  • I(rm): information extracted from product m’s linked page.
  • J: GPT-5-mini’s binary decision, 1 for satisfied and 0 for not satisfied.

Table 4 reports this score on a 0–100 scale. The paper defines M symbolically and does not state a fixed list length in the full text. §3.4.2

Target-finding hit ratio

A returned product is a hit when it matches the target’s brand and product type, regardless of checklist fit. Each Target Finding variant has 50 tasks, so Table 5’s ratio uses those 50 cases for that variant. This metric isolates locating the requested item from personalizing the result. §5.2 and Table 5

Meta-evaluation measures

Alignment accuracy asks whether a representation identifies the higher-rated item in a low-rated/high-rated pair (250 samples). Cohen’s κ measures chance-corrected agreement; Spearman’s ρ measures rank correlation. The human comparison uses 50 instances, each independently labeled by two people. §4.1, Tables 2–3

What the comparisons support

  • Finding a target is not enough. The best hit-ratio cell is 0.88 for SeeAct on Explicit Title, while its personalization score for that same scenario is 37.13. The two metrics measure different outcomes. Tables 4–5
  • Open Exploration remains weak. Every system scores between 13.56 and 21.20 without an explicit product query. §5.1
  • Aesthetic-driven curation drops sharply for several systems. Claude falls from 29.66/29.08 on high/low involvement to 20.58 on aesthetic-driven selection; ChatGPT is the exception among the search systems at 27.55. Table 4
  • Valid-page conditioning changes the comparison. In the subset where every curated URL is valid and accessible, autonomous agents often post large gains; for example, Agent-E rises from 19.75 to 54.02 on TF/Explicit Title. This is a selected successful-run subset, not the full benchmark. Appendix C, Table 7

Limitations and reading cautions

  • Live-web results can drift. Prices, availability, reviews, page layouts, and links change. The benchmark deliberately uses live pages, so later reruns need not reproduce the same evidence.
  • The profiles are constructed. They begin with real Amazon histories and reviews, but GPT-5-mini writes the narrative context and checklist. The benchmark therefore tests alignment to an inferred profile, not a new preference statement written directly by the user. §3.3
  • The score depends on two model stages. A web-enabled model extracts product facts, then GPT-5-mini judges checklist satisfaction. Extraction errors and judge errors can affect the score. The human study supports the full method, but κ = 0.7320 is not perfect agreement. §4.1.2
  • Failure labels need their stated denominator. Table 6’s 24.76% for ChatGPT Search is its overall failure rate. The text separately says it hallucinates approximately 20% of product references. Neither number is a universal rate for all systems. §5.2, Table 6
  • Successful-case scores are conditional. Table 7 excludes responses unless every curated URL is valid and accessible. Use Table 4 for overall performance and Table 7 to study behavior after successful attribution. Appendix C
  • Causal explanations are hypotheses. Claims about why one architecture fails, or why exploration helps, come from behavior and error analyses rather than randomized interventions.

Takeaway

AgenticShop’s main result is a separation between product discovery and personalized curation. Agents can sometimes locate the requested brand and product type, yet still satisfy only a minority of the user-specific criteria. The benchmark’s distinctive contribution is an evidence-grounded way to measure that gap across target search, comparison shopping, and query-free exploration.
