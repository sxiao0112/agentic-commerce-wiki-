The Automated but Risky Game: Modeling and Benchmarking Agent-to-Agent Negotiations and Transactions in Consumer Markets

Contribution

The paper proposes a comprehensive experimental framework to investigate agent-to-agent negotiation and transaction scenarios where both consumers and merchants authorize AI agents to automate their negotiations. The authors construct a dataset of 100 real-world products spanning three categories (electronic devices, motor vehicles, real estate), each with retail prices and estimated wholesale costs. They benchmark nine advanced language models (GPT series, Qwen series, DeepSeek series) as both buyer and seller agents across different budget configurations. The work identifies that negotiation performance substantially correlates with model general capabilities, and reveals four key behavioral anomalies that translate into tangible financial risks: constraint violation, excessive payment, negotiation deadlock, and early settlement. As a preliminary mitigation approach, the authors demonstrate that reinforcement learning based prompt optimization can reduce out-of-budget errors for vulnerable populations.

Research Question

What happens when consumers and merchants delegate their negotiations and decision-making to AI agents with varying capabilities, and what are the risks and performance characteristics of fully automated agent-to-agent negotiation in realistic consumer markets?

Task

The negotiation setting is formulated as an information-incomplete zero-sum game between buyer and seller agents. Each agent receives different background information: the buyer knows the retail price, product features, and their budget constraint; the seller knows the retail price, wholesale cost, and product features. Both agents aim to optimize the final transaction price within feasibility constraints. Buyer agents initiate the conversation and are constrained to not exceed their budget. Seller agents aim to keep prices near retail while maintaining above wholesale cost. A judge model determines whether a deal is accepted, rejected, or negotiation continues, with a maximum of 30 rounds.

Data

The dataset consists of 100 real consumer products obtained from trustworthy sources with actual retail prices and product specifications. Wholesale costs were estimated using GPT-4o prompted with market norms and industry information. The dataset is distributed across three categories: 40% motor vehicles, 30% electronics, 30% real estate. For each of the 100 products, the authors sampled 50 products randomly, running five trials per product across five distinct budget levels (high: 1.2 times retail; mid: between retail and wholesale; low: 0.8 times wholesale; below wholesale). A total of 9 models were evaluated, each playing both buyer and seller roles against every other model, with 5 budget configurations per pairing, generating extensive negotiation data.

Metrics

Price Reduction Rate (PRR) quantifies a buyer's ability to negotiate discounts from the retail price, also reflecting seller's success in resisting reductions. Relative Profit (RP) measures total profit achieved relative to the lowest-profit seller (GPT-3.5). Deal Rate reports the proportion of successful negotiations. Profit Rate averages profit margins per successful transaction. Anomaly-specific metrics include Out-of-Budget Rate (OBR): proportion of negotiations where accepted price exceeds buyer budget; Out-of-Wholesale Rate (OWR): proportion where accepted price falls below wholesale cost; Overpayment Rate (OPR): proportion of deals where final price exceeds retail price; Deadlock Rate (DLR): proportion reaching maximum rounds without agreement.

Baselines

The evaluation encompasses nine models: OpenAI's GPT series (o3, o4-mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-3.5), DeepSeek series (DeepSeek-v3, DeepSeek-R1), and Qwen series (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B). The paper treats DeepSeek-R1 as the strongest performer and GPT-3.5 as the weakest for economic impact analysis. Models are evaluated on their inherent negotiation behaviors with minimal prompt tuning.

Results

Substantial disparities in negotiation capability emerge across models. O3 demonstrates the strongest overall performance with highest price retention as seller and greatest discounts as buyer. GPT-3.5 consistently underperforms. Models with higher parameter counts within the Qwen2.5 family exhibit better negotiation outcomes, supporting scaling laws in this domain. Negotiation capacity correlates very strongly with general task performance on MMLU (r=0.93), substantially with mathematical ability MATH (r=0.87), and moderately with model size (r=0.53). Four key behavioral anomalies are identified: (1) Constraint Violation: weaker models frequently accept deals violating budget constraints, with Qwen2.5-7B breaching budget limits in over 10% of cases and reaching 18.5% out-of-wholesale violations under low budgets; (2) Excessive Payment: buyer agents sometimes overpay beyond retail price when budgets are disclosed early, with most models showing overpayment rates above zero except DeepSeek and strongest GPT models; (3) Negotiation Deadlock: weaker models struggle to recognize negotiation futility, with Qwen2.5-7B showing highest deadlock rates under low-budget conditions; (4) Early Settlement: buyer agents with higher budgets accept seller offers more readily, demonstrating an average 8.9% gap in price reduction rates between highest and lowest budget settings. A multi-armed bandit RL-based prompt optimization reduces out-of-budget errors dramatically (from 18.4% to 1.3% for Qwen2.5-7B) while showing limited gains on overpayment and deadlock. Economic impact analysis reveals clear disparities: weak buyers facing strong sellers overpay by approximately 2%, and weak sellers lose up to 14.13% profit against strong buyers. Model choice asymmetrically affects outcomes, with seller model choice having significantly larger impact on negotiation results than buyer choice.

Conclusion

The paper demonstrates that fully automated agent-to-agent negotiation in consumer markets is an inherently imbalanced game where parties using less capable models face systematic economic disadvantages. While automation can enhance efficiency, deploying AI agents in consumer settings poses significant risks stemming from LLM behavioral anomalies, including budget constraint violations, excessive payments, deadlocks, and suboptimal settlement strategies. These issues are not merely academic failures but translate into tangible financial losses for both individual users and aggregate market welfare. Users should exercise substantial caution when delegating high-stakes negotiation and transaction authority to AI agents, and practitioners should consider human-in-the-loop approaches, especially for financially vulnerable populations. Future work should move beyond risk diagnosis toward comprehensive mitigation strategies that jointly optimize performance and safety in real-world agent-mediated commerce platforms.
