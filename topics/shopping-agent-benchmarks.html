Shopping and e-commerce agent benchmarks

Shopping benchmarks ask whether an AI agent can turn a request into a correct product choice or commerce action. This review connects findings across ten studies. It also identifies what the studies measure differently.

How to read this review. A shared finding means that several studies report a related problem in their own tests. It does not mean that they tested the same system or found the same effect size. The linked paper pages explain each experiment, metric, source version, and limit.

How the benchmarks evaluate an agent

The studies cover product search, comparison shopping, personalized recommendations, remembered preferences, and customer support. Their tests generally follow the pattern below, but their actions and scoring rules differ.

Recurring findings

Finding a plausible product is easier than meeting the full request

Several studies test whether an agent can satisfy multiple requirements together. WebShop checks product attributes, options, type, and price. DeepShop combines product features, filters, and sorting preferences. ShoppingBench adds tasks involving background facts, several items from one seller, and coupons within a budget. WebMall asks agents to gather and compare offers across shops.

These tests report incomplete matches even when the agent makes useful progress. WebShop's agent trained from human demonstrations succeeds less often than human experts, with a large gap in selecting options such as size or color. DeepShop reports that browser-operating agents struggle more as requests become complex. ShoppingBench's failure audit identifies mismatched attributes as its largest category. WebMall exposes difficulty collecting the full set of relevant offers. Together, these findings show a recurring problem with completing every required part of a request in the tested settings.

The scoring rules matter. WebShop gives partial credit as well as a separate complete-success rate. DeepShop requires every applicable feature, filter, and sorting requirement to pass. ShoppingBench uses different all-or-nothing checks for each kind of shopping request. WebMall distinguishes exact completion from how many correct offers the agent returns. A useful but incomplete answer can therefore receive a positive score and still fail the task.

Finding information does not guarantee that the recommendation is justified

ShoppingComp, AgenticShop, and Shopping Companion examine whether a recommendation follows from available evidence. They approach this question differently: open-web product discovery and reports, personalized product checklists, and shopping informed by earlier conversations.

ShoppingComp separates finding the right products from writing a valid explanation. Its deep-research systems achieve broader product finding in some comparisons, but their explanation-validity scores are below those of GPT-5 and GPT-5.2. AgenticShop separately checks whether a returned page is valid and relevant, then whether the product meets the shopper's checklist. Shopping Companion's audit of 69 failed GPT-5 cases identifies unverified product attributes in 27 cases. In this review, these results distinguish finding information from verifying a recommendation and explaining it.

Each study adds a different perspective. ShoppingComp uses expert requirements and safety cases. AgenticShop grounds its checks in product-page evidence and behavior-derived shopper profiles. Shopping Companion tests a two-stage process: recover preferences from past conversations, then search and inspect products. Their results do not establish that one verification method will work across all shops.

Personalization is an additional goal, not another name for task success

The personalization studies ask whether an agent can complete the shopping task while respecting the shopper's preferences. ShopperBench evaluates ten shopper types and also scores how the agent's actions follow the assigned type. AgenticShop scores personalized product recommendations against detailed checklists. Shopping Companion requires agents to recover relevant preferences from earlier sessions.

These studies show why a task-success score alone misses part of the result. In ShopperBench, the task-focused policy has the highest task-success score, while the policies adapted to the shopper type have higher scores for how closely their actions follow the assigned shopper type. Those policies also take more steps. AgenticShop distinguishes locating a product of the requested brand and type from satisfying the full personalization checklist. Shopping Companion distinguishes retrieving preference attributes from meeting every product and preference requirement. These are related goals with separate measurements.

The studies use different forms of shopper information: assigned personas, behavior-derived profiles, and conversation histories. Their findings support testing preference use explicitly. They do not show that these forms are interchangeable, or that the same effect holds for real shoppers outside the tested setups.

Reliable completion and safe advice need different checks

ECom-Bench tests whether an agent resolves customer-support cases through dialogue and commerce tools. It measures both success on one attempt and success across repeated attempts. For GPT-4o, reported pass1 is 44.03%, while pass3 is 16.98%. Here, pass3 estimates the chance that all three attempts succeed; it does not mean that at least one attempt succeeds.

ShoppingComp instead includes shopping questions with product-related hazards and checks a dedicated safety requirement. Its tested models remain below expert safety results. These studies contribute complementary evidence: completing a support case consistently and recognizing a product hazard are distinct tasks. A low repeated-success score is not itself evidence of unsafe advice, and a safety score does not establish reliable checkout or support performance.

What the studies add about improvement and evaluation

Training can improve one tested workflow without proving general capability

ShoppingBench and Shopping Companion both test training a smaller model for their own tools and tasks. ShoppingBench trains Qwen3-4B from successful GPT-4.1 tool-use examples, then adds reward-based training. Its trained model reaches 48.7% overall exact task success, close to GPT-4.1's 48.2% on that benchmark. Shopping Companion tests rewards for individual memory and product tools. In its controlled comparison, those rewards improve average task success over reward-based training that scores only the final outcome.

The distinction between metrics is important even during training. In WebShop, additional reward-based training improves the average partial-credit score but slightly lowers complete success. The two newer training studies use different tasks and rewards, so their percentages cannot be combined into a common estimate of improvement.

The interface and evaluator affect what a benchmark result tells us

WebMall compares page observations: screenshots, a structured list of page elements called an accessibility tree, and combinations with memory. Its paper reports weak screenshot-only results in the tested shopping tasks. This adds evidence about the agent's interface, alongside the requirement-handling problems measured by DeepShop. It does not establish that images are unhelpful for every shopping task.

ShopGym adds a different question: how closely do controlled copies of shops reproduce results on live storefronts? The authors report that aggregate agent success broadly tracks between live shops and three paired shop copies. They do not report a correlation coefficient, so this is narrower evidence than a quantified prediction of live performance. WebShop also tests transfer to real sites, but those tests cover navigation through an adapter rather than autonomous real purchases. Both contributions make the scope of the environment part of the evidence.

Scoring can also introduce uncertainty. WebShop's exact feature matching can miss synonyms. DeepShop checks agreement between an AI model that grades the result and one human on a sample. ShopperBench reports persona scores but does not fully specify the underlying judge formulas and repetition counts. The paper pages state these limits instead of filling the gaps with assumptions.

What remains unresolved

The shared evidence concerns particular catalogs, websites, models, tools, shopper descriptions, and scoring rules. It does not yet establish a single measure of shopping competence. The studies leave open how well improvements transfer across changed stores and tools, and how to evaluate complete success, justified recommendations, preference use, repeated reliability, and safety together.
