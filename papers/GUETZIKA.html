Shopping Companion

Can an agent recover preferences hidden across long conversation histories, then use them to verify real products and obey bundle constraints?

  • Yu et al.
  • arXiv:2603.14864v3
  • 27 May 2026
  • cs.CL
  • benchmark + training method

Pinned primary full text: v3 · version record · DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2603.14864

Question and answer

Research question

Where do long-horizon shopping agents fail, and can per-tool rewards improve them?

The task joins cross-session preference retrieval with grounded product search. The paper asks whether feedback on each memory and product call can improve a small agent beyond final-answer feedback alone.

Supported answer

Process supervision helps, but bundle shopping remains hard.

For Qwen3-4B, adding tool-wise reward to outcome-level RL raises average preference accuracy from 69.5% to 72.5% and average task success from 59.5% to 63.5%. The add-on success rate is still 43.0%.

Evidence: Section 6.2, Table 2.

Exact task and data

Single product

Retrieve relevant preferences from long-term memory. Find and verify one product that matches them.

Add-on deal

Retrieve preferences for several products. Select a bundle while meeting product count, same-shop or voucher conditions, and budget constraints.

Benchmark construction

1,000 queries: 500 per task. The split is 800 training queries and 200 test queries. The product pool contains 1,298,797 Lazada items. Each item can expose title, price, category, brand, description, and structured attributes.

The authors generate instructions and preference dialogues from sampled products, remove conflicts against LongMemEval histories, insert the dialogue as a sparse preference session, and have two e-commerce experts check resolvability, product retrievability, and preference consistency.

Histories average 56.4 sessions and 106,380 tokens. Wanted attributes average 2.1 for single-product queries and 5.6 for add-on deals.

Evidence: Section 4; Appendix A.1.

Agent flow

  1. Search memorymem_search retrieves candidate turns or sessions.
  2. Verify memorymem_view opens full sessions before a preference is accepted.
  3. Confirm preferencesStage 1 emits preferences. A simulator may give no hint, a low hint, or a high hint.
  4. Search productsproduct_search returns candidates and can filter by shop and price.
  5. Verify and answerproduct_view checks detailed attributes; Stage 2 returns the recommendation.

Memory retrieval uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings and cosine similarity. Product retrieval uses BM25 over 1.29M items and returns at most 50 candidates. See Appendix B.1.

Metrics and denominators

Stage 1

Preference accuracy (Acc.)

“Fraction of reference preference attributes correctly retrieved by the agent.” For one response with F reference attributes and m matched attributes:

attribute fraction = m / F

The paper does not say whether Table 2 aggregates these fractions as a macro-average per query or as one micro-average over all attributes. It also does not publish uncertainty intervals.

Stage 2

Success rate (Succ.)

A test query counts as 1 only when every applicable criterion passes: correct product count, user need satisfaction, long-term preference consistency, and budget feasibility for add-on deals.

Succ. = successful test queries / all test queries

Section 5.2 states that evaluation sampled 100 queries per task in the 200-query test set.

Training rewards

Outcome reward: Stage 1 uses (q₁ + m₁ + b·c₁) / (1 + F + b·N). Stage 2 uses (p₂ + q₂ + m₂ + b·(n₂ + u₂)) / (2 + F + b·(N + 1)). Here b is 1 for add-on deals and 0 otherwise; N is reference bundle size.

Per-call tool reward: for a memory call, relevant returned or viewed session indices divided by all indices returned or viewed; for a product call, reference product IDs divided by all retrieved or viewed product IDs. Semantic matching is used when exact product ID matching is unavailable.

Rtool = (Σ per-call reward) / number of tool calls; 0 if no tool calls

Total: outcome reward + mean tool reward + format reward, with unit weights.

Definitions and equations: Section 4, Evaluation Metrics; Section 5.3, Equations 6–12.

Experiments, models, and controls

Zero-shot models

Closed: GPT-5, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, Qwen3-Max.

Open: DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2-Instruct, Gemma-3-27B, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3-4B.

Training ladder

Backbone: Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507. The comparison is LoRA SFT; LoRA plus outcome-level RL; and LoRA plus outcome-level and tool-wise RL.

SFT starts from 2,948 GPT-4.1 rejection-sampled step examples. RL uses 800 training queries split into 1,600 stage instances.

Workflow controls

On GPT-5, the paper compares an oracle context, one-stage execution, and two-stage execution with no, low, or high corrective hints. Low and high hints are used only in evaluation.

Evaluation checks

Five LLM judges evaluate four closed-model agents. Their pairwise score-vector Pearson correlations give OPC 0.9547 ± 0.0289. Eight annotators double-annotate 200 samples: iPAR is 0.8950 and GPT-5-judge-to-human-consensus PAR is 0.9497.

The paper names these agreement measures but does not provide their formulas.

Version discrepancy: v3 Appendix D.1 says “train for 5 epochs” after describing the stage-split RL data. Appendix D.2 separately states SFT uses 3 epochs and RL uses 2.6 epochs. This card reports both statements and uses the method-specific 2.6 value only when describing RL.

Setup: Section 6.1; details: Appendix D; judge checks: Appendix E.

What failed

Denominator

The authors inspect all 200 GPT-5 zero-shot test trajectories, then classify the 69 failed trajectories. Percentages below use 69, not 200.

Largest failure

27 / 69 = 39.1%

Unverified attributes: the agent infers a feature from a title without confirming it through product_view.

Remaining categories: same-shop alignment 11/69 (15.9%); multiple SKUs in a single-product task 9/69 (13.0%); product ID or sourcing errors 8/69 (11.6%); preference hallucination 7/69 (10.1%); insufficient tool calls 7/69 (10.1%).

These categories come from a two-stage LLM attribution and clustering process. The paper does not report independent human validation of the taxonomy.

Evidence: Appendix F.2, Table 7.

Distinctive contribution

The paper connects three elements in one evaluation: cross-session memory, search over a large real product catalog, and interactive correction between preference extraction and shopping. Its training contribution mirrors that design: it rewards the quality of intermediate memory and product calls, using benchmark annotations and traces instead of new per-call human labels.

The clearest empirical comparison is narrow: on one 4B backbone, tool-wise reward improves both reported final metrics and shortens average trajectories relative to outcome-only RL. This supports the reward design within the paper’s tool environment; it does not establish that the same reward transfers to other domains.

Limits and reading cautions

Stated by the authors

  • Add-on deals remain difficult: even the trained model reaches only 43.0% Succ.
  • Tool-wise rewards depend on this domain’s tools and gold signals. Generalization may need substantial redesign.
  • Cross-session memory raises privacy, profiling, fairness, and spending-influence risks.

Scope visible in the design

  • Instructions and preference dialogues are synthetic, although experts verify each instance.
  • Products come from one platform and an authorized research snapshot.
  • Primary scores depend on an LLM judge. The paper checks judge ranking consistency and human agreement, but reports no uncertainty for model scores.
  • Acc. aggregation is not fully specified, and the training epoch statements conflict.

Author limits and ethics: Conclusion, Limitations, and Ethical Considerations. Design cautions are labeled as such and derive from Sections 4 and 6 plus Appendices D–E.

Brief definitions

Long-term memory (LTM)
Conversation history from earlier sessions that may contain user preferences.
Preference grounding
Connecting a claimed preference to evidence in the user’s history.
LoRA
A parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that updates small low-rank matrices.
GRPO
The reinforcement-learning algorithm used to optimize sampled groups of trajectories.
LLM-as-judge
A language model that scores recommendations against task criteria and reference information.
Oracle
An evaluation condition that supplies the ground-truth preference-relevant sessions as context.
