Vertical tacit collusion in AI-mediated markets

Contribution

The paper identifies vertical tacit collusion as a novel market failure in AI-mediated consumer markets. Platforms controlling product rankings and sellers controlling product descriptions independently learn to exploit documented AI cognitive biases through standard profit-maximizing behavior without any communication. Using multi-agent simulation calibrated to empirical measurements of large language model biases, the work demonstrates that joint exploitation produces consumer harm more than double what would occur if strategies were independent. The paper introduces the gatekeeper mechanism whereby platform ranking acts as a binary switch determining whether seller manipulation harms or helps consumers. Unlike horizontal algorithmic collusion requiring coordination, vertical tacit collusion requires no agreement, emerges from aligned incentives around a shared exploitable target, and evades traditional antitrust detection frameworks.

Research Question

How do platforms and sellers in AI-mediated markets independently learn to exploit AI agent cognitive biases, and what is the magnitude and mechanism of consumer harm that emerges from these aligned but uncoordinated strategies?

Method

The research formalizes AI-mediated markets as a repeated game with platforms, sellers, and AI agents. The platform selects information architecture (bid weight determining quality-ranking versus bid-ranking trade-off, endorsement rules, decoy placement). Sellers choose manipulation intensity (keyword insertion, anchoring language, framing effects) and bid level. The AI agent computes perceived utility incorporating four bias channels: position effects, endorsements, seller manipulation effectiveness, and decoys. Platform and sellers learn through Q-learning, a model-free reinforcement learning algorithm. The parameterization for bias effects is calibrated to empirical findings from the LLM cognition literature. The AI agent selects products via softmax over utilities, capturing essential decision-making properties of deployed LLM shopping agents.

Setup

Simulations model a stylized marketplace with six sellers varying in quality, one platform, and one AI shopping agent representing consumers. Sellers have quality levels in {0.90, 0.75, 0.60, 0.45, 0.30, 0.20} with corresponding cost tradeoffs calibrated to consumer electronics gross margins. Each simulation runs 20,000 rounds, with outcomes measured over the final 40% to ensure convergence. Results are averaged across 100 independent trials with different random seeds. Four experimental conditions isolate the mechanism: fair baseline with quality-based ranking and no seller manipulation; platform-only learning with sellers remaining naive; seller-only learning with fixed quality-based platform ranking; joint learning where both adapt simultaneously. Bias parameters are calibrated to empirical measurements: position effects follow documented 15-25 percentage point accuracy differences between first and middle positions; endorsement effects reflect platform badge impacts; manipulation susceptibility follows evidence of LLM vulnerability to description optimization; decoy effects follow asymmetric dominance patterns documented in instruction-tuned LLMs.

Results

In the fair baseline, consumer surplus averages 0.303. When both platform and sellers learn to exploit AI biases, consumer surplus falls to 0.191, representing a reduction of 37.1% (P < 0.0001), observed in all 100 trials. Platform-only learning reduces consumer surplus by 27.0% relative to baseline. Seller-only learning increases consumer surplus by 9.6%, because higher-quality sellers earn higher margins and invest more in description optimization, making manipulation correlate with quality rather than substitute for it when the platform maintains quality-based ranking. When both parties learn simultaneously, joint harm (37.1%) substantially exceeds the sum of independent effects (17.4% predicted under independence). This 19.7 percentage point difference represents pure strategic complementarity, harm existing only because both parties learn simultaneously. The gatekeeper threshold analysis reveals a binary mechanism: seller manipulation helps consumers at bid weights from 0 to 0.67 (effects ranging from -7.1% to -9.0%), but only complete abandonment of quality-based ranking (bid weight = 1.0) enables catastrophic harmful exploitation (+69.2%). Position bias is the dominant exploitation channel, producing 29.4% of consumer harm alone and accounting for 79% of the full model's 37.1% effect. Without position bias, manipulation-only produces -10.3% harm, meaning consumers benefit. Complementarity persists across learning algorithms (Actor-Critic, REINFORCE, Exp3), parameter variations, extended simulations to 100,000 rounds, and heterogeneous consumer populations. A falsification test with debiased agents reduces complementarity by 96%, confirming that exploitable AI biases are necessary. All 100 trials reach substantial complementarity within 3,500 rounds on average.

Conclusion

Vertical tacit collusion represents a distinct form of market failure that emerges from independent profit-maximizing behavior without coordination among parties at different levels of digital marketplaces. The superadditive harm arises because platforms control the information architecture determining which products occupy bias-triggering positions while sellers control product presentation determining conversion rates. This creates a gatekeeper mechanism where the platform's ranking choice acts as a binary switch between pro-consumer and anti-consumer outcomes. The mechanism requires only that actors learn from feedback, a feature common to modern optimization approaches. AI shopping agents with exploitable biases and market structures where platforms separate information architecture from quality-based ranking create the conditions for vertical tacit collusion. Current antitrust frameworks designed to detect communication-based coordination are inadequate to address this phenomenon, creating a regulatory gap as AI shopping agents reach mainstream deployment. Policy interventions could include platform liability for information architecture exploitability, robustness standards for commercial AI shopping agents, transparency requirements for AI-mediated information environments, or structural remedies preventing simultaneous control of marketplace infrastructure, ranking algorithms, and affiliated AI shopping agents.

Related Work

Platform economics and two-sided markets

Two-sided markets and platform competition literature has analyzed how platforms balance buyer and seller interests. The paper notes that this literature does not account for the case where AI agents mediate consumer decisions, fundamentally changing platform incentives around serving consumers honestly. The framework builds on foundations in platform economics but introduces a key modification relevant to AI-mediated commerce.

Algorithmic pricing and horizontal collusion

Artificial intelligence, algorithmic pricing, and collusion research demonstrates how competing sellers learn to coordinate prices through algorithmic optimization. Autonomous algorithmic collusion under Q-learning examines strategic behavior under sequential pricing. Adversarial competition and collusion in algorithmic markets extends this to competitive dynamics. The paper distinguishes vertical tacit collusion from horizontal settings by showing that vertical market structures lack the coordination problems that characterize horizontal collusion among competitors occupying the same market position.

Large language model cognitive biases

Extensive research has documented cognitive-like biases in large language models. Lost in the middle studies show LLMs exhibit strong position biases in retrieval tasks with accuracy differing by 15-25 percentage points between first and middle positions. Serial position effects are confirmed across GPT, Llama-2, T5, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3, and Gemini-Pro model families. The ACES benchmark evaluated AI shopping agents making actual product choices and found substantial asymmetries in selection patterns. The Magentic Marketplace study documented severe first-proposal bias creating 10-30x advantages for response speed over quality. Beyond position effects, LLMs exhibit anchoring biases, susceptibility to framing effects, confirmation bias, keyword manipulation, and decoy effects consistent with human decision-making patterns. Broader evaluations found LLMs exhibit cognitive biases in 40% of tested scenarios across model families. Research on using cognitive psychology to understand large language models applies behavioral economics frameworks to LLM decision-making. Cognitive bias in decision-making with LLMs in EMNLP 2024 findings documents bias emergence in LLM reasoning. Instructed to bias shows instruction-tuned language models exhibit emergent cognitive bias.

Discrete choice modeling and utility theory

The logit choice model specification draws on frontiers in econometrics, with the softmax distribution corresponding to random utility maximization under Type I extreme value errors. This provides a standard foundation in discrete choice analysis for modeling decision-making under uncertainty.

Advertising as information and signaling

Advertising as information and Advertising as a signal establish foundational theory that advertising can convey product quality. Product badges and consumer choice on digital platforms extends this to modern platform endorsement mechanisms. Research on advertising spending and perceived quality and empirical examination of advertising as signal of product quality connects advertising investments to quality signaling. The paper uses signaling theory to explain why seller manipulation increases with seller quality under quality-based ranking.

Asymmetric dominance and decoy effects

Adding asymmetrically dominated alternatives documents decoy effects and violations of choice regularity. Decoy effects in choice experiments and contingent valuation extends asymmetric dominance analysis to various choice settings. The paper implements decoy effects consistent with patterns documented in both human and LLM decision-making.

Broader delegation and intermediary bias

Federal antitrust policy establishes the legal framework for competition enforcement. Competition law reviews enforcement approaches and legal standards. These works establish the antitrust context showing that current frameworks require evidence of communication or facilitating practices to prove collusion. The paper argues that vertical tacit collusion evades these frameworks entirely because harm emerges from aligned incentives rather than agreement.

AI alignment and adversarial robustness

Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback introduces alignment approaches focused on ensuring AI systems follow instructions and avoid harmful outputs. Constitutional AI proposes harmlessness from AI feedback. Recent analysis on irrationality and cognitive biases in large language models examines how AI systems may be irrational in ways diverging from studied human heuristics. Towards understanding sycophancy in language models documents LLM susceptibility to user framing. The paper argues that traditional AI alignment approaches provide limited protection in adversarial economic environments and that economic deployment requires adversarial robustness rather than instruction-following alone.

Market regulation and AI governance

The EU AI Act establishes risk-based classification frameworks for AI systems. Red teaming language models to reduce harms documents evaluation protocols. The paper proposes these regulatory approaches as templates for requiring robustness certification of commercial AI shopping agents. FTC endorsement guidelines requiring disclosure of material connections provides a template for transparency requirements in AI-mediated information environments.

AI shopping systems and e-commerce

ChatGPT shopping research and Amazon Rufus documentation describe deployment of AI shopping agents to hundreds of millions of consumers. Google Shopping AI mode extends AI shopping to search contexts. The Magentic Marketplace environment provides an open-source testbed for studying agentic markets. Multi-agent reinforcement learning for market making and market structure dynamics ground the simulation in realistic e-commerce contexts.
