Embodied retail store environments

Embodied retail environments are 3D simulations of physical stores. Agents move through the store, inspect products, and manipulate objects to complete shopping tasks.

Evidence scope. This page covers one collected paper: Sari Sandbox. The paper has one arXiv version, dated August 1, 2025. The authors also state that the paper was accepted at the ICCV 2025 RetailVision workshop. Results on this page refer to that version.

What this area studies

This area studies agents that act in a simulated physical store. The agent uses visual observations and physical actions. This setting differs from a shopping website, where an agent acts through page controls and a document object model.

The Sari Sandbox paper connects retail tasks to three established embodied AI tasks:

  • Visual exploration: The agent moves and gathers information about the environment.
  • Visual navigation: The agent moves to a goal location.
  • Embodied question answering: The agent navigates and answers a question about the environment.

Retail tasks can combine these abilities with product handling. A task can ask an agent to find an item, pick it up, scan it, or compare product information.

Current evidence

The collected evidence comes from Gajo et al. (2025). The authors present Sari Sandbox and SariBench.

Sari Sandbox

  • It is a Unity-based 3D convenience-store simulation.
  • It contains 250 products in 11 food categories.
  • It provides three store layouts.
  • It includes shelves, refrigeration cabinets, and a self-checkout counter.
  • It stores product data such as price, weight, ingredients, nutrition facts, allergens, and manufacturing origin.
  • It provides a Python client and a Unity server. They communicate through WebSocket messages.
  • It supports VR interaction for people and API control for agents.

The authors describe the environment as photorealistic and high fidelity. These are the authors’ descriptions. The paper reports an OCR test on product labels, but it does not provide a broad independent measure of realism.

SariBench

SariBench groups shopping tasks into three levels:

  • Easy: Find and pick up one product.
  • Average: Retrieve a product and use checkout. These tasks add memory and task execution.
  • Difficult: Compare product information and take a conditional action. These tasks add decision-making and comprehension.

The paper reports 100 human demonstration videos from 20 participants. It says each participant received three easy tasks, two average tasks, and two difficult tasks. The recordings include head pose, hand pose, grip state, and hovered or held items at 10 frames per second. The researchers also transcribed participants’ spoken thoughts.

Reported results

Human and agent performance

The paper compares people with one proof-of-concept agent on easy tasks. The agent uses Gemini 2.5 Pro preview 05-06. It uses a ReAct-style loop and does not use model fine-tuning.

  • People completed easy tasks in 47 to 73 seconds on average across the three layouts.
  • Human completion rates for easy tasks ranged from 88.88% to 100%.
  • The agent completed successful easy tasks in 420 to 780 seconds on average.
  • Agent completion rates ranged from 33.33% to 68.63%.
  • The longest reported agent average was about 16 times the matching human average.

The paper defines agent success as completion within 45 minutes. It defines failure as exceeding that limit or getting lost. The authors call the agent a solvability proof of concept. They do not present it as an optimized benchmark.

Environment and label tests

The paper reports 26.73 frames per second for Layout 1, 23.23 for Layout 2, and 35.14 for Layout 3. The test computer used an Intel i7-8700 CPU and an NVIDIA GTX 1080 GPU. These results are hardware-specific.

The paper also tests PaddleOCR on product label text. It reports 0.986 precision, 0.943 recall, and a 0.014 character error rate. The authors note weaker results for rotated and stylized text.

What the results support

  • The environment can support human VR interaction and API-controlled agent interaction.
  • The proof-of-concept agent can complete some easy tasks.
  • People performed the reported easy tasks faster and more often than the tested agent.
  • The published agent evaluation does not cover average or difficult tasks.

The results do not establish performance for other models, other hardware, or physical stores. The paper evaluates one environment and one agent design.

Links to related wiki topics

  • Shopping and e-commerce agent benchmarks also cover search, comparison, and checkout. Those benchmarks may use web interfaces instead of physical movement.
  • Web agent environments simulate browser-based work. Embodied retail environments simulate movement and object interaction in a store.

Open questions

  • How well do agents perform on the average and difficult SariBench tasks?
  • Which errors come from perception, navigation, manipulation, memory, or planning?
  • How much can model choice, context design, or fine-tuning improve performance?
  • Can the environment reach a stable 60 frames per second on the target class of computers?
  • How well do results transfer to other store layouts, product sets, and physical retail settings?
  • How should later work reconcile the paper’s reported counts? The paper reports 100 demonstration videos, 108 assigned human evaluation tasks, and seven assigned tasks per participant.

Contradictions and limits

No direct contradiction appears among the collected papers because this topic currently contains one paper.

The paper calls Sari Sandbox a retail-specific contribution and says retail simulation is underexplored. It does not establish that no other retail simulator exists. The authors discuss earlier retail digital twins and the Future Convenience Store Challenge.
