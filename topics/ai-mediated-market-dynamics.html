Economics, manipulation, and collusion in AI-mediated markets

How autonomous AI shopping and trading agents reshape market outcomes, including the biases, manipulation, collusion, and instability that emerge when agents mediate economic decisions.

Definition

AI-mediated markets are consumer markets where AI agents select products, negotiate, and transact on behalf of users, creating a demand surface that differs from human-centric commerce. Allouah et al., 2025 characterize this as a fundamentally different demand surface, with market shares that are volatile, highly model-dependent, and susceptible to upstream model updates acting as exogenous demand shocks. Affonso, 2026 formalizes such a market as a repeated game between platforms, sellers, and an AI agent, where the platform sets the information architecture and sellers choose manipulation intensity.

A central concept is that AI agents are not perfectly rational economic actors but exhibit systematic, exploitable biases. Allouah et al., 2025 document strong position biases, choice homogeneity, sensitivity to platform endorsements, and penalization of sponsored tags across frontier models. Affonso, 2026 treats these biases as a shared exploitable target, modeling four bias channels: position effects, endorsements, seller manipulation effectiveness, and decoys. Bansal et al., 2025 name first-proposal bias as the dominant behavioral pattern distorting agentic markets, where speed of response beats quality.

Seller-side manipulation is a concrete attack on these biases, not just an abstract lever. Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024 demonstrate strategic text sequences: token strings optimized by an adversarial attack and inserted into a product's information to push that product to the top of an LLM's recommendations. They frame this as the AI-era successor to search engine optimization, but potentially more effective, because the vendor manipulates the model's choice directly rather than a ranking signal.

Vertical tacit collusion is the market failure that Affonso, 2026 identifies: platforms controlling rankings and sellers controlling descriptions independently learn to exploit AI biases through profit maximization, with no communication or agreement between them. This is distinguished from horizontal algorithmic collusion, which requires coordination among competitors at the same market position.

Economic alignment is the property Karten et al., 2026 define: the capacity of agentic systems to preserve market stability and integrity while protecting human participants against exploitation and fraud. They frame it as distinct from general reasoning capability and orthogonal to model size.

State of the area (synthesis)

The papers here converge on one finding from different angles: deploying AI agents into markets introduces failure modes that uncoordinated, individually rational behavior produces. The clearest documentation of the underlying vulnerability comes from Allouah et al., 2025. Their ACES framework runs randomized experiments on a controllable mock platform and causally isolates how product attributes and platform levers move agent choice. Frontier agents assign large utility premiums to top-row placement, with position changes yielding 5-7 fold swings in selection probability. The direction of position bias differs sharply across providers, so much that GPT-4.1 and GPT-5.1 exhibit almost opposite preferences. Agents penalize sponsored tags but reward platform overall-pick endorsements with a 3-5x selection lift. These biases persist in headless settings with no images and resist correction by explicit prompting, suggesting they are deep model properties rather than surface artifacts.

Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024 show how a seller can weaponize this kind of vulnerability directly. Using the Greedy Coordinate Gradient adversarial algorithm, they optimize a strategic text sequence and embed it in a target product's listing in a catalog of ten coffee machines ranked by Llama-2. The sequence elevates a product that never appeared in recommendations to the top choice within roughly one hundred iterations, and lifts a product that ranked second to first with high probability. Optimizing the sequence under random permutations of the product list makes the advantage robust to ordering. This gives the abstract seller-manipulation channel a concrete mechanism: the listing optimization that Allouah et al., 2025 flag as a vulnerability and that Affonso, 2026 treats as a strategic variable can be realized as an adversarial text attack on the model itself, which connects this market-level concern to the search-manipulation methods in generative-engine-optimization.

Bansal et al., 2025 reach a complementary behavioral finding in a two-sided market where assistant agents face service agents. First proposals achieve selection rates of 60 to 100 percent while third proposals see near-zero selection, a 10-30 fold advantage for speed over quality. They also surface a paradox of choice: expanding the consideration set can reduce welfare, with some models dropping 44-65 percent. On manipulation, their result is more reassuring than alarming. Frontier models resist traditional psychological tactics like authority, social proof, and loss aversion, keeping payments to manipulative businesses low. Smaller open-source models remain vulnerable, and some frontier models show degradation under strong prompt injection. This places the manipulation-resistance question alongside the security framing in agent-security-and-prompt-injection.

Affonso, 2026 takes the documented biases as given and asks what market structure does with them. The answer is the paper's core claim. When both the platform and sellers learn to exploit AI biases, consumer surplus falls 37.1 percent, far above the 17.4 percent predicted if the two effects were independent. This 19.7 percentage point gap is strategic complementarity, harm that exists only because both parties adapt at once. The mechanism is a gatekeeper: the platform's ranking choice acts as a binary switch. Under quality-based ranking, seller manipulation correlates with quality and can even help consumers, but abandoning quality-based ranking unlocks catastrophic harm. Position bias is the dominant channel, accounting for 79 percent of the full effect, and a falsification test with debiased agents removes 96 percent of the complementarity. The biases ACES measures are thus the necessary fuel for the collusion Affonso models, a direct evidential link that Affonso draws by citing the ACES selection asymmetries and the first-proposal bias from Bansal et al., 2025.

Karten et al., 2026 shift the unit of failure from consumer harm to market integrity. Their Agent Bazaar simulation exposes two emergent failure modes. In the Crash, firms undercut each other below unit cost until the market collapses; at baseline, Gemini 3 Flash and GPT 5.4 show bankruptcy rates above 0.65 while Claude Sonnet 4.6 self-organizes to a viable equilibrium. In the Lemon Market, a single deceptive principal controls multiple Sybil seller identities to flood the market with fraudulent listings, raising its revenue share as cluster size grows. In-context harnesses help but stay fragile under harder conditions. Targeted REINFORCE++ training is more robust, and a trained stabilizing firm acts as a market anchor whose benefit spills over to untrained competitors, raising their survival from 0 to 68 percent. Their Economic Alignment Score shows alignment is orthogonal to model size: an RL-finetuned 9B model ranks first among 20 models, ahead of much larger ones.

Two cross-cutting tensions organize the area. First, the same behavioral phenomenon recurs across independent environments: position and first-proposal biases appear in Allouah et al., 2025, Bansal et al., 2025, and as the engine of harm in Affonso, 2026, which strengthens confidence that these are real properties of deployed agents rather than artifacts of any one testbed. Second, the failures arise without coordination. Affonso's collusion needs no agreement, and Karten's crash and lemon dynamics emerge from individually rational play, both echoing the algorithmic-collusion literature each cites where Q-learning sellers reach supra-competitive outcomes without communication. The shared implication is governance, and a recurring lever is auditing. Allouah et al., 2025 call for algorithmic economic auditing and public agent-choice dashboards, Affonso, 2026 argues current antitrust frameworks built to detect communication cannot reach tacit vertical exploitation, and Karten et al., 2026 treat economic alignment as a trainable safety target. Auditing the curation layer that shapes what users see is a related concern: Zhao et al., 2026 use persona-conditioned multimodal agents as a scalable, privacy-preserving way to audit recommender filter bubbles, measuring how content diversity narrows and how strongly platform algorithms resist a user's attempt to escape. The marketplace environments that host these dynamics are developed further in agentic-marketplaces, and the negotiation behaviors that drive some of them connect to agent-to-agent-negotiation.

Papers in this field

  • Affonso, 2026 — identifies and models vertical tacit collusion in AI-mediated markets, showing platforms and sellers independently learn to exploit AI cognitive biases and produce super-additive consumer harm that evades traditional antitrust detection.
  • Allouah et al., 2025 — develops ACES, a provider-agnostic framework auditing how frontier AI shopping agents choose products, revealing strong position biases, choice homogeneity, model-dependent volatility, and exploitable vulnerability to seller listing optimization.
  • Karten et al., 2026 — introduces Agent Bazaar and the concept of economic alignment, exposing Algorithmic Instability and Sybil Deception failure modes in LLM agents and showing targeted RL training mitigates both orthogonally to model size.
  • Bansal et al., 2025 — builds Magentic Marketplace and empirically shows frontier agents can approach optimal welfare under ideal search yet exhibit severe first-proposal bias, paradox-of-choice welfare loss, and variable manipulation resistance.
  • Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024 — demonstrates that strategic text sequences optimized via an adversarial attack and inserted into a product listing can manipulate an LLM's e-commerce recommendations to push a target product to the top, threatening fair market competition.
  • Zhao et al., 2026 — builds PersonaAct, persona-conditioned multimodal agents that simulate short-video users to enable scalable, privacy-preserving auditing of filter bubbles, revealing platform-dependent content narrowing and escape potential.

Contradictions

  • Manipulation resistance of frontier agents: Bansal et al., 2025 find frontier models robustly resist traditional psychological manipulation and keep payments to manipulative businesses low, whereas Allouah et al., 2025, Affonso, 2026, and Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024 find frontier agents remain substantially exploitable through seller-side listing optimization, information-architecture manipulation, and adversarial strategic text sequences. The tension is one of attack surface: psychological persuasion tactics in conversation versus structural and adversarial exploitation of position, endorsement, and description channels.

Open questions / gaps

  • The mechanistic source of agent biases is unresolved: whether position and choice biases come from pre-training, post-training, the vision encoder, or model drift, and how agent behavior compares to human baselines on identical tasks Allouah et al., 2025. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Real platforms and sellers use more sophisticated optimization than Q-learning, and the analysis omits the retrieval stage and some bias channels, so modeled vertical-collusion harm may be conservative; the feasibility and market impact of the proposed regulatory remedies remain empirically untested Affonso, 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Economic alignment is shown in simulations that abstract away order books, differentiated goods, and correlated demand; robustness to distribution shift from continuously adapting opponents rather than fixed base models, and self-play as a more robust training alternative, remain untested Karten et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Current findings come from static markets where neither agents nor environment learn from history; temporal dynamics, repeated adaptation, and hybrid human-AI markets are flagged as essential extensions before deployment Bansal et al., 2025. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Adversarial strategic text sequences expose a need for safeguards: countermeasures to detect and prevent listing-level manipulation of LLM recommenders, and ethical guidelines for AI search optimization that preserve marketplace integrity and fair competition, remain to be developed Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Recommender-auditing methods are constrained by data scale and observation length: filter-bubble auditing has been validated only on a small set of users and platforms over short windows, leaving longer-horizon bubble evolution and the effect of diversity-injection or friction interventions untested Zhao et al., 2026.
