Economics, manipulation, and collusion in AI-mediated markets

AI agents can search, compare, recommend, negotiate, or buy for people. Their choices can change demand. Platforms and sellers can also shape the information that these agents receive.

Scope. This page summarizes controlled experiments and simulations. Most studies do not observe live markets at scale. Results apply to the tested models, prompts, interfaces, products, and simulation rules.

What is an AI-mediated market?

In an AI-mediated market, an AI agent takes part in an economic decision for a person or firm. The agent can act as a buyer, seller, service provider, or market participant.

The papers on this page study three levels:

  • Agent choice: How an agent selects among products or proposals.
  • Strategic influence: How a platform or seller changes what the agent sees.
  • Market outcomes: How many agents interact under market rules.

These levels are related, but they are not interchangeable. A measured choice bias does not by itself prove market-wide harm. A simulated market failure does not show that the same failure already occurs in a live market.

What the evidence shows

Agent choices depend on model and interface

Allouah et al. (2025) introduce ACES, a controlled shopping environment. Their current arXiv version is v3 from December 2025. The authors randomize product and platform features to measure how they affect agent choice.

  • The tested agents often concentrate demand on a small set of products.
  • Model updates can change the selected products and the direction of position effects.
  • Position effects remain in text-only tests. Instructions to ignore position reduce some effects but do not remove all of them.
  • The tested agents penalize sponsored labels and favor platform endorsements.
  • Seller-side description changes sometimes increase market share. The effect varies by model and product category.

These results support continuous testing. They do not establish one stable preference pattern for all shopping agents.

Proposal order can outweigh service quality

Bansal et al. (2025) introduce Magentic Marketplace. The environment connects assistant agents for consumers with service agents for businesses.

Frontier models approach high welfare under favorable search conditions in the reported experiments. Performance falls as the market becomes larger. All tested models also show a strong first-proposal bias. The paper reports a 10 to 30 times advantage for response speed over quality in its setting.

The paper also tests manipulation. Frontier models resist some traditional persuasion tactics better than smaller models. This result has a narrow scope. It does not test every form of structural, textual, or prompt-based manipulation.

Listing text can manipulate recommendations

Kumar and Lakkaraju (2024) test a catalog of fictitious coffee machines with Llama 2. They add an optimized strategic text sequence to a product page.

The added text makes the target product more likely to appear as the top recommendation. The result demonstrates a specific attack in a controlled setting. It does not measure prevalence on current commercial systems.

This attack differs from ordinary persuasion. It optimizes text against a model. It also differs from the simple seller description edits in ACES. See generative engine optimization and search manipulation for related work.

Platforms and sellers can create super-additive harm in simulation

Affonso (2026) models a platform, sellers, and an AI shopping agent. The platform selects rankings. Sellers select how strongly they manipulate descriptions. The simulation calibrates agent biases from prior empirical work.

The paper calls the result vertical tacit collusion. The platform and sellers do not communicate or make an agreement. They independently optimize profit. Joint exploitation causes more consumer harm than the sum of the separately modeled effects.

This is a simulated market mechanism. It is not evidence of a specific legal violation. The paper argues that antitrust tools focused on communication may miss this type of aligned behavior.

Multi-agent simulations reveal instability and deception

Karten et al. (2026) introduce Agent Bazaar and define economic alignment as the ability of agentic systems to preserve market stability and integrity.

The paper studies two simulated failures:

  • The Crash: Seller agents undercut prices until firms can fail.
  • The Lemon Market: One deceptive actor controls several seller identities and offers low-quality goods.

Results vary by model. Prompt-based harnesses improve some outcomes but remain fragile under harder conditions. Targeted reinforcement learning improves the authors’ Economic Alignment Score in the tested environments. A trained 9B model ranks above the larger tested models on that score.

The simulations omit many features of real markets. These include order books, differentiated goods, correlated demand, and continuously adapting opponents.

How the findings fit together

  • Repeated observationPosition or order affects choices in both ACES and Magentic Marketplace. The studies use different tasks and measures.
  • Concrete attackKumar and Lakkaraju show that optimized listing text can change one model’s recommendations.
  • Market mechanismAffonso uses measured agent biases as inputs to a simulated platform-and-seller game.
  • System riskAgent Bazaar tests instability and deception when autonomous agents interact over time.

Do not combine the estimates directly. Each paper uses a different market, model set, outcome measure, and experimental design.

A real tension in the literature

Are frontier agents resistant to manipulation?

The answer depends on the attack surface.

  • Bansal et al. report that frontier agents resist several conversational persuasion tactics in their service marketplace.
  • Allouah et al. report sensitivity to position, endorsements, and description changes.
  • Kumar and Lakkaraju report successful model-targeted manipulation with strategic text.
  • Affonso assumes exploitable bias in a calibrated simulation and tests its market effects.

These findings do not form a direct contradiction. The studies test different interventions. Resistance to one persuasion tactic does not establish resistance to ranking design, listing optimization, or adversarial text.

Auditing and mitigation

The papers propose or test several responses:

  • Run repeated, randomized audits across models and model versions.
  • Publish agent choice profiles or other monitoring results.
  • Test platform rankings, badges, and search rules as separate variables.
  • Evaluate seller content for model-targeted manipulation.
  • Measure market stability, integrity, welfare, and profitability together.
  • Train agents for economic outcomes and test them under harder market conditions.

These are research directions. The papers do not establish a complete governance standard. Recommender-system audits are related, but they measure a different market layer. Zhao et al. (2026) use persona-conditioned multimodal agents to audit short-video filter bubbles. Their initial study uses two users and 1,719 interactions, so its platform findings need broader validation.

Open questions

  • Which parts of training or inference produce position and order effects?
  • How do agent choices compare with human choices in the same interface?
  • Do results persist during retrieval, long tasks, and repeated purchases?
  • How often do sellers use model-targeted listing techniques in live markets?
  • How do adapting buyers, sellers, and platforms affect long-term outcomes?
  • Which audits predict consumer harm outside a controlled environment?
  • Which interventions improve outcomes without creating new manipulation channels?
  • How should law distinguish tacit optimization, platform design, and an agreement?
