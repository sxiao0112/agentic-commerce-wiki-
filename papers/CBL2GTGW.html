Magentic Marketplace: An Open-Source Environment for Studying Agentic Markets

Contribution

The authors design and implement Magentic Marketplace, an open-source simulation environment for studying how LLM-powered agents behave in two-sided agentic markets where Assistant agents represent consumers and Service agents represent businesses. They develop a REST API architecture supporting the full transaction lifecycle from search and discovery through negotiation to transaction execution. Using this environment, they conduct systematic empirical studies of agent welfare, manipulation vulnerability, and behavioral biases under varying market conditions, and open-source the platform to enable further research on agentic market design.

Research Question

As autonomous LLM agents increasingly mediate economic decisions and transactions on behalf of users, several critical questions arise: How effectively can agents discover and transact with one another in realistic market conditions? How do market design decisions (such as search mechanisms) impact agent performance at scale? How does current AI agent technology compare to ideal agentic behavior and non-agentic market baselines? How vulnerable are agents to strategic manipulation and behavioral biases in competitive marketplace settings?

Method

The authors implement Magentic Marketplace using an HTTP/REST client-server architecture with three core endpoints: register (agent registration), protocol (dynamic capability discovery), and action (execution of marketplace operations). The action endpoint supports five core actions: Search (discovery of service agents), Send Text Messages (communication), Send Order Proposals (structured offers), Send Payments (transaction completion), and Receive (asynchronous message handling). This minimal three-endpoint design enables extensibility while supporting the complete economic lifecycle. The architecture separates marketplace state from agent state and maintains backward compatibility as new capabilities are added.

Setup

The authors conduct experiments in fully synthetic data domains (Mexican restaurants and contractors) to enable controlled experimentation and safe exploration of agent behaviors. The data generation pipeline consists of three stages: creating a universe of items with price distributions, synthesizing customers with specific menu/service requests and amenity constraints, and generating candidate businesses with varying price structures and feature completeness. They evaluate two market scales: small (33 customers, 99 businesses) and medium (100 customers, 300 businesses). The utility model defines consumer utility as value (dollars) for having a need met minus the price paid; the value is calibrated as two times the average price of desired items. Agents are evaluated on frontier proprietary models (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Flash) and open-source models (GPT-OSS-20b, Qwen3-14b). All experiments use 5 independent runs with means and standard deviations reported.

Results

First-proposal bias emerges as the dominant behavioral pattern distorting marketplace dynamics across all tested models: first proposals achieve selection rates between 60-100%, while third proposals see near-zero selection, creating a 10-30 fold advantage for response speed over quality. Frontier models (GPT-4.1, Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Flash) approach optimal welfare when provided with perfect search results (access to the three best-matching businesses), suggesting that with accurate discovery and effective communication, agentic markets can substantially reduce information asymmetries. Performance degrades sharply with realistic lexical search conditions; for example, GPT-4o's consumer welfare declines 4.3% when search results increase from 3 to 100 options. The consideration set size experiments reveal a paradox of choice effect where more search results paradoxically reduce welfare, with some models (Sonnet-4, GPT-5) showing welfare declines of 44-65%. Frontier models (GPT-4.1, Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Flash) demonstrate robust resistance to traditional psychological manipulation tactics (authority, social proof, loss aversion), maintaining stable performance and low payments to manipulated businesses (typically under 1.0 out of 3.0). However, some vulnerability to strong prompt injection attacks appears in certain models (Gemini-2.5-Flash shows reduced performance under strong prompt injection in the Mexican restaurant scenario). In contrast, GPT-4o, GPT-OSS-20B, and Qwen3-4B demonstrate significant vulnerability across multiple manipulation vectors including traditional psychological appeals and prompt injection. Qwen3-4B proves particularly susceptible to both authority and social proof tactics. Position bias in search results shows variable effects: frontier models demonstrate near-uniform selection across positions, but Qwen3-4B exhibits severe position bias, selecting the third-listed business at 57-67% rates compared to the 33% expected under random selection. Open-source models show more varied results overall, with competitive performance under perfect search but notable degradation under realistic lexical search conditions.

Conclusion

Two-sided agentic markets show promise for improving consumer welfare by reducing information asymmetries through coordinated agent-to-agent interaction, but systematic behavioral variations across models and significant vulnerabilities warrant careful market mechanism design before real-world deployment. The severe first-proposal bias identified across all models reveals that marketplace performance may be fundamentally constrained not by agent reasoning ability but by temporal dynamics: businesses gain more competitive advantage from fast response than from quality or pricing improvements. The paradox of choice in agent decision-making suggests that expanding consumer options does not necessarily improve outcomes and may instead overwhelm agent reasoning capabilities or cause agents to satisfice on early proposals. Frontier models demonstrate stronger reasoning and manipulation resistance than smaller models, with performance gaps widening as market complexity increases. The authors emphasize that end-to-end simulation environments are essential for understanding emergent behaviors and designing safe, efficient agentic marketplaces before commercial deployment.
