Two-sided agentic marketplaces and the agentic economy

Simulation environments and empirical studies of markets where LLM agents transact on both sides, acting for consumers and for businesses.

Definition

A two-sided agentic marketplace is a market where both sides are represented by autonomous LLM agents that interact directly with each other. In Magentic Marketplace, Assistant agents represent consumers and Service agents represent businesses, and the two coordinate across the full transaction lifecycle from search and discovery through negotiation to transaction execution Bansal et al., 2025. The motivating premise is that agent-to-agent interaction can reduce information asymmetries, because agents can converse to explore the full range of available options rather than relying on static listings Bansal et al., 2025.

Agent Bazaar frames the same setting through the lens of Economic Alignment, defined as the capacity of agentic systems to preserve market stability and integrity while protecting human participants against exploitation and fraud Karten et al., 2026. This treats market-level health as a distinct property of multi-agent systems, separate from any single agent completing its task well Karten et al., 2026.

Both works build marketplaces as controlled simulations rather than live deployments. Magentic Marketplace uses an HTTP/REST client-server architecture with three endpoints (register, protocol, action), where the action endpoint supports Search, Send Text Messages, Send Order Proposals, Send Payments, and Receive Bansal et al., 2025. Agent Bazaar formalizes its markets as a Partially Observable Stochastic Game and enforces partial observability through a consumer discovery limit that restricts how many sellers a buyer can see Karten et al., 2026.

A recurring design choice in both is grounding scenarios in familiar commercial settings. Magentic Marketplace uses synthetic Mexican restaurant and contractor domains Bansal et al., 2025. Agent Bazaar uses a business-to-consumer market inspired by Amazon and a consumer-to-consumer market inspired by eBay Karten et al., 2026.

State of the area (synthesis)

The central empirical message of this area is that agentic markets can work well under ideal conditions but break in characteristic ways once realistic frictions are added. Magentic Marketplace shows that frontier models approach optimal welfare when given perfect search results, suggesting agent-to-agent markets really can reduce information asymmetries Bansal et al., 2025. The promise is conditional, not automatic.

The failures are structural rather than reasoning failures. Magentic Marketplace identifies first-proposal bias as the dominant distortion across every model tested: first proposals are selected 60-100% of the time while third proposals are selected almost never, giving response speed a 10-30 fold advantage over offer quality Bansal et al., 2025. This implies businesses compete on being fast rather than on price or fit. The same study finds a paradox of choice: enlarging the consideration set can reduce welfare, with some models losing 44-65% Bansal et al., 2025. More options overwhelm agent decision-making rather than improving it.

Agent Bazaar adds a second class of failure that emerges from interaction among agents rather than from a single agent's choices. It identifies two emergent failure modes. The first is Algorithmic Instability, or The Crash, where firms undercut each other below unit cost until the market collapses Karten et al., 2026. The second is Sybil Deception, or The Lemon Market, where one deceptive principal controls many coordinated seller identities to flood the market with fraudulent listings Karten et al., 2026. Both are individually rational but collectively destructive, and capable frontier models do not reliably avoid them.

The two studies agree that model behavior is heterogeneous, but they probe different axes of it. Magentic Marketplace finds frontier models resist traditional psychological manipulation such as authority, social proof, and loss aversion, while smaller models like GPT-4o, GPT-OSS-20B, and Qwen3-4B are far more vulnerable to those tactics and to prompt injection Bansal et al., 2025. Agent Bazaar finds frontier models themselves diverge sharply on stability: Gemini 3 Flash and GPT 5.4 crash with bankruptcy rates above 0.65, while Claude Sonnet 4.6 self-organizes to a viable equilibrium with no bankruptcies Karten et al., 2026. A model that is robust on one dimension is not automatically robust on another.

A notable claim from Agent Bazaar is that economic alignment is orthogonal to model size. Its RL-finetuned 9B model achieves the top Economic Alignment Score among 20 models, outranking much larger ones, and small models can outperform larger ones on the same metric Karten et al., 2026. This contrasts with the general pattern in Magentic Marketplace, where frontier models broadly outperform smaller ones on welfare and manipulation resistance Bansal et al., 2025. The two are not in direct conflict, because they measure different things: task-level welfare and manipulation resistance versus a market-stability-and-integrity composite. Together they suggest scale helps an agent act well for its own principal but does not by itself produce healthy markets.

On interventions, the two papers take complementary stances. Magentic Marketplace stops at diagnosis and argues that careful market mechanism design is needed before deployment, pointing to discovery quality and the temporal dynamics behind first-proposal bias as the levers Bansal et al., 2025. Agent Bazaar tests fixes directly. Its in-context harnesses, the Stabilizing Firm and the Skeptical Guardian, improve outcomes but stay fragile under harder conditions Karten et al., 2026. Its REINFORCE++ training with an adaptive curriculum produces more robust behavior, and a trained stabilizing firm acts as a market anchor whose stability spills over to untrained competitors, lifting their survival from 0% to 68% Karten et al., 2026.

Agent Bazaar's use of competition incentives to shape agent behavior has a parallel outside the marketplace-as-object-of-study framing. Bittensor Agent Arenas treats a deliberately engineered incentive-aligned agent arena not as a market to be kept healthy but as a generator of training data: ORO Subnet 15 deploys the ShoppingBench benchmark as a Bittensor subnet where many independent teams are continuously paid to discover policies competitors have not found Bansal et al., 2026. The arena combines race mechanics, per-trajectory scoring by an LLM reasoning judge, and rotating held-out problems with leak-cluster guards, and the resulting judged multi-turn trajectories are filtered by a structural-quality gate and distilled into a Qwen3-4B shopping agent Bansal et al., 2026. Where Agent Bazaar asks whether incentive structures keep a market stable, this work asks whether a competitive incentive structure can be engineered to emit incentive-aligned, judged trajectories as a side effect Bansal et al., 2026. Both treat the incentive mechanism, rather than the individual agent, as the primary design surface.

Papers in this field

  • Bansal et al., 2025 — Magentic Marketplace, an open-source REST-based simulation environment for two-sided agentic markets, used to show that frontier agents approach optimal welfare under perfect search but exhibit severe first-proposal bias and paradox-of-choice effects under realistic conditions.
  • Karten et al., 2026 — Agent Bazaar, a multi-agent marketplace simulation that defines Economic Alignment, exposes Algorithmic Instability and Sybil Deception as emergent failure modes, and shows that in-context harnesses help but targeted RL training with an adaptive curriculum produces robust, size-orthogonal alignment.
  • Bansal et al., 2026 — Bittensor Agent Arenas, which reframes a deliberately engineered incentive-aligned agent competition (ORO Subnet 15 running ShoppingBench) as a trajectory-data primitive, using race mechanics and per-trajectory LLM judging to produce judged trajectories that are structurally filtered and distilled into a Qwen3-4B shopping agent.

Open questions / gaps

  • Markets in Magentic Marketplace are static: neither agents nor environment learn from history, so dynamic markets where agents and users interact repeatedly and adapt remain unstudied Bansal et al., 2025. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Human-in-the-loop and hybrid markets with both human and AI participants are highlighted as essential but beyond current scope Bansal et al., 2025. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Simulations abstract away order books, differentiated goods, and correlated demand, and real market complexity likely amplifies the identified failure modes Karten et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Robustness to distribution shift is untested, since trained agents face fixed base-model opponents rather than continuously adapting ones, and self-play training is suggested but unexplored Karten et al., 2026. Mirrored in open-questions.
  • Whether incentive-aligned competition arenas can serve as a trajectory primitive for task surfaces that lack a deployed subnet or evaluation infrastructure is unaddressed, as is generalization beyond index-based agentic commerce to live website navigation Bansal et al., 2026.
