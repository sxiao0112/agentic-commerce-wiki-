DRIP-R: A Benchmark for Decision-Making and Reasoning Under Real-World Policy Ambiguity in the Retail Domain

Contribution

DRIP-R is a benchmark for evaluating LLM-based conversational agents operating under realistic, ambiguous domain policies. The authors introduce three main contributions: the DRIP-R benchmark itself, comprising 400 scenarios (40 tasks paired with 10 customer personas) grounded in real Amazon return policy; a multi-dimensional evaluation framework spanning policy adherence, dialogue quality, behavioral alignment, task-resolution alignment, and interest alignment with calibrated LLM judges; and empirical findings showing that frontier models fundamentally disagree on resolution outcomes in policy-ambiguous scenarios, confirming that policy ambiguity poses a systematic challenge to agent decision-making.

Research Question

How do LLM-based customer service agents reason, make decisions, and interact when confronted with inherently ambiguous domain policies where no single correct resolution exists? Do different models consistently converge on the same policy interpretation, and how do policy ambiguities manifest in conversational trajectories and final resolutions?

Task

Order Return scenarios: customers request to return items from an e-commerce purchase and negotiate resolution with a customer service agent. Each scenario involves at least one ambiguous policy clause that affects resolution. Tasks are stratified by complexity (low/medium/high), ambiguity type (vagueness, incompleteness, coordination ambiguity, missing condition, semantic ambiguity), and diversity across scenario setup, policy coverage, product types, and item condition complexity.

Data

40 high-quality tasks derived from 500 candidates, created by prompting GPT-4o-mini to generate realistic return scenarios that depend on identified policy ambiguities and elicit multi-turn dialogue. Tasks specify order timeline, item-level conditions, delivery status, and customer circumstances. 10 customer personas drawn from Persona-Hub and augmented with Big Five personality traits, communication styles (rapport vs. report-oriented, direct vs. indirect, linear vs. non-linear), language styles (Joos's Five Clocks model), age, income range, and purchase history. Full dataset: 400 scenarios (40 tasks × 10 personas). Ambiguities extracted from Amazon's return policy using GPT-4.1 with human validation (IAA 84.62%, Cohen's κ = 0.58). Realistic task content confirmed through manual verification by expert reviewers; for 10 representative tasks, human annotators independently labeled plausible outcomes, showing substantial disagreement and validating task ambiguity (mean exact agreement 0.10, Jaccard partial agreement 0.46).

Metrics

Five top-level evaluation dimensions with multiple subdimensions, each scored on a 1-5 Likert scale: - Policy Adherence: policy support per-turn, at conversation level, and at resolution level; reasoning quality; say-do gap between stated and executed commitments. - Dialogue Quality: conversation consistency, consistency drift, resolution-oriented responses, verbosity, argument-execution fidelity, resolution description fidelity. - Behavioral Alignment: agent and customer tone/behavioral consistency with assigned personas; values alignment using Schwartz value theory (conformity, benevolence, self-direction, security, universalism, power). - Task-Resolution Adherence: whether outcome addresses customer reason and aligns with task constraints (usage, timing, bundle details). - Interest Alignment: customer goal alignment (measured as fraction of explicit customer goals achieved) and company interest alignment (policy-compliance score). Evaluation implemented via LLM-as-Judge using five parallel judges with multi-stage calibration (DPFS + G-Eval); achieves mean bias 0.33 and adjacent agreement 0.84 on held-out test set.

Baselines

Four LLM models evaluated: two API-only (GPT-5-2025-08-07, GPT-4.1-2025-04-14) and two open-source (gpt-oss-120b, Qwen3-35B-A3B). Customer simulator uses GPT-4o-mini for consistent, cost-efficient turns. All scenarios run through each model under identical settings (temperature 0.7 for agents, max 20 turns). Evaluation uses GPT-5.4-2026-03-05 as judge. Conversation follows Dec-POMDP formalism: agent has tool-calling access; user simulator does not.

Results

Cross-model resolution agreement analysis reveals fragmentation on policy interpretation: only 4.2% of scenarios show full agreement (all four models produce the same outcome); 82.4% show partial agreement (2–3 models converge); 13.3% show full disagreement (each model different). Policy adherence trajectories differ by outcome type and model: GPT-5 maintains uniformly high policy support (≥4.83) regardless of outcome, while Qwen3 and OSS-120B show drift after conversation midpoint, with refund-granting outcomes dropping further than escalations. Behavior-decision gap (in-conversation policy adherence minus resolution policy adherence) is positive across all ambiguity types, largest for coordination ambiguity (0.29), indicating agents' in-conversation reasoning exceeds resolution consistency. User persona neuroticism and extraversion correlate with outcomes favoring customer goals (ρ = 0.109, p < 0.0001 and ρ = 0.077, p = 0.0016 respectively); agreeableness correlates inversely (ρ = −0.054, p = 0.0259), raising fairness concerns about simulated customer service rewarding certain personality traits. Agent persona strongly anchors outcomes: DIRECT agents deny refunds most frequently, VERY_HELPFUL agents least (χ²(4) = 59.03, p < 0.001), though model determines what replaces denials (full refund vs. escalation vs. partial).

Conclusion

Policy ambiguities are an inherent feature of real-world deployment yet remain largely absent from existing agent benchmarks, which rely on purpose-built, unambiguous policies. DRIP-R establishes a foundation for evaluating how LLM-based agents navigate realistic ambiguity through grounding in Amazon's return policy, systematic ambiguity extraction, and multi-dimensional evaluation spanning policy adherence, dialogue quality, behavioral alignment, and interest alignment. Empirical findings demonstrate that frontier models fundamentally disagree on policy-ambiguous scenarios, confirming ambiguity as a genuine, systematic challenge. Results also reveal that conversational policy adherence exceeds resolution consistency, certain ambiguity types (especially coordination ambiguity) are harder for agents to resolve consistently, and agent and user persona significantly shape outcomes. The authors acknowledge limitations: the benchmark is currently scoped to retail only, relies on a single primary policy document, and would benefit from evaluation on a larger model set. Future work should expand to other high-stakes domains (healthcare, legal, airline) and incorporate broader policy sets.
